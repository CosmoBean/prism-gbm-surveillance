# Tone and word choice: the author's corrections (2026-11-20)

These rules come from the author's own corrections to the abstract and Introduction. Each is paired with the case
that produced it. They govern every section.

## What the author rejected, and why

| The author objected to | Example | What replaced it |
|---|---|---|
| A gimmicky hook or wordplay | "whether a model reads the tumour or the calendar" | "a single AUC cannot show what drives a model's predictions" |
| A rhetorical "banger" at the end; sounding defensive or arguing | "Timing did not carry over to other centres, but the model did"; "Studies should report what a score rests on beside its AUC" | Plain statements of the results, with no closing moral |
| A sentence that makes no sense when read literally | "Because the scores are kept separate, PRISM shows how much of each ranking comes from…" | "For each scan, PRISM reports what its score rests on: …" |
| A connective that claims logic that isn't there | "PRISM therefore works from conventional MRI…" | "PRISM works from conventional MRI…" |
| A connective the reader doesn't need | "was likewise evaluated on a single held-out set" | "was evaluated on a single held-out set" |
| A judgement word used for plain data | "the evidence available at every routine follow-up" | "both part of every routine follow-up" |
| A crude or tacked-on clause | "…, which is what PRISM uses." | PRISM becomes the subject of its own sentence |
| A vague placeholder word | "score the images, the clinical history and both" | "and the two combined" |
| An awkward aside | "On MU-Glioma-Post, the public cohort used here, …" | "…, the cohort used in this study, …" |
| A fact dropped in with no stated purpose | "a radiomics model reported an AUC of 0.80 on 30 held-out patients" | A sentence that says why the fact is there (it was evaluated on a single held-out set) |
| A list of components where the reader wanted to know what PRISM does for them | Five abstract sentences each naming a component | One sentence on what it does, and the findings |
| Jargon instead of meaning | "80% split-conformal set" | "the outcomes it cannot rule out, chosen so that they include the true outcome for 80% of scans" |
| A "therefore", "so" or "because" that overstates the argument | (several) | State the fact; let the reader connect it |
| One paragraph carrying several ideas | Model evaluation, shortcuts and saliency maps in one paragraph | One idea per paragraph, each leading to the next |

## Rules

1. **Say it plainly.** A short declarative sentence with a concrete subject (PRISM, the model, the scan, the
   checker) and an active verb. Put the claim first.
2. **No rhetoric:**
   - no rhetorical questions;
   - no wordplay or hooks;
   - no closing morals;
   - no "X, not Y" slogans;
   - no colon reveals ("The answer: …");
   - no triplets for rhythm;
   - no quotable last lines.
3. **No defensive tone.** Do not argue with an imagined critic inside a sentence. Do not stack qualifiers. State a
   limitation once, where it applies, in plain words.
4. **Connectives only when the logic is real.** "Therefore", "so", "because", "thus", "hence" and "as a result" must
   mark a true consequence. Cut "likewise", "notably", "importantly", "indeed", "in fact", "crucially", "of course",
   "simply", "clearly", "it is worth noting".
5. **Every sentence must have a purpose the reader can see.** If a reader would ask "why are you telling me this?",
   either say why in the same sentence or cut it.
6. **Plain words over judgement words.**
   - Data are data, not "evidence".
   - Use "uses", not "leverages"; "shows", not "demonstrates"; "about", not "approximately", unless exactness
     matters.
   - Do not use "robust", "powerful", "novel", "comprehensive", "seamless", "sophisticated" or "careful".
7. **Name the technology exactly, and say what it does in one plain clause.** Do not pile technical terms into one
   sentence.
8. **No vague placeholders.** "Both", "this", "these", "such" and "it" must have an obvious referent. Otherwise name
   the thing again.
9. **No awkward asides or stacked parentheticals.** At most one aside per sentence.
10. **Keep sentences readable.** If a sentence needs to be read twice, split it.
