\begin{abstract}
Machine learning models for glioma follow-up MRI report high accuracy, but a single AUC cannot show what drives a
model's predictions. We built PRISM (Progression Reading with Independent Scores and Measurements),
running on one local computer: a Swin UNETR network trained on BraTS data segments each scan, three logistic regressions score it from its image measurements, its clinical history and the two
combined, each with a conformal prediction set, and a local large language model writes a
report whose every number is checked against its source. On MU-Glioma-Post, the combined model reached an AUC of
0.82. Without retraining, it reached 0.77 at LUMIERE and 0.86 at UCSF-ALPTDG. For each scan,
PRISM reports what its score rests on: the image and clinical-history scores, whether they agree, and how the models
performed on comparable scans.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
glioma surveillance, brain tumour segmentation, large language models, conformal prediction, external validation
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

Gliomas make up about a quarter of all primary brain tumours, and glioblastoma alone about half of the malignant
ones \citep{ostrom2023}. Even with surgery, radiotherapy and temozolomide, median survival is about 15 months
\citep{stupp2005}. After treatment, patients are followed with MRI every few months, and each scan feeds a decision:
continue treatment, scan again sooner, or change treatment.

Reading these scans is hard because treatment can look like progression. Pseudoprogression follows chemoradiotherapy
in 20--30\% of patients \citep{wen2010}, more often when the MGMT promoter is methylated \citep{brandes2008}, and in
one cohort half of the patients whose first scan after radiotherapy looked progressive had pseudoprogression
\citep{taal2008}. Clinicians therefore read each scan against the time since treatment, and RANO 2.0 asks for a
repeat scan or histology before progression is called within 12 weeks of radiotherapy \citep{wen2023}.

Advanced MRI helps in some studies but has not solved the problem. In a meta-analysis of 30 small studies, 13 at high
risk of bias, diffusion and perfusion MRI reached pooled AUCs of 0.90--0.93 \citep{taylor2022}, and adding them
improved a radiomics model in 61 patients \citep{kim2019}. In 105 patients with suspected pseudoprogression, however,
they did not significantly improve on clinical data and MGMT status \citep{leone2023}. PRISM works from conventional
MRI and the clinical record, both part of every routine follow-up.

Machine learning models for this decision are mostly built on the image alone and tested on a single split of one
cohort. Studies of treatment response have small samples and a high risk of bias \citep{booth2022}, 29\% of 51 glioma
radiomics studies were validated externally \citep{park2020}, and 3 of 20 studies of automated response assessment
considered clinical characteristics \citep{shi2025}. Single splits of small cohorts give unstable estimates
\citep{an2021}, and most deep learning algorithms for radiological diagnosis lose performance on external data
\citep{yu2022}. A radiomics model published on MU-Glioma-Post, the cohort used in this study, was
evaluated on a single held-out set of 30 patients (AUC 0.80) \citep{christodoulou2026}.

A single AUC does not show what drives a model's predictions. Models learn shortcuts, signals that predict the label
without being what the reader cares about \citep{geirhos2020,zech2018,badgeley2019}. In this setting, a model of
clinical data alone matched one using MRI on an external test set \citep{gomaa2025}, and the interval between scans
strongly affected a model built on the RANO rules \citep{tarek2026}. The useful measure is what a model adds beyond
what a clinician already knows \citep{pencina2008,beaulieujones2021}. Saliency maps drawn after the fact do
not show this reliably \citep{arun2021,saporta2022}, so a model that is interpretable by design is preferable
\citep{rudin2019}.

The image measurements themselves depend on how the tumour is outlined. After surgery and radiotherapy the scan
contains a resection cavity and treatment changes, which make segmentation harder, and the BraTS 2024 challenge was
set up for this setting \citep{deverdier2024}. Experts disagree about the quality of the same glioma segmentation
\citep{hoebel2024}, so measured volumes depend on whose outlines are used.

A score is also more useful when it states its uncertainty. Conformal prediction turns a score into a set of possible
outcomes that contains the true one at a chosen rate \citep{angelopoulos2023}, although the guarantee does not hold
within subgroups or under distribution shift \citep{mehrtens2023}.

Finally, the result reaches the clinician as a report, and reports vary. Of 220 European centres, 60\% assessed
follow-up scans qualitatively, 27\% applied RANO and 23\% used a reporting template \citep{thust2018}. After the
structured BT-RADS system was adopted \citep{weinberg2018}, reports mentioned bevacizumab and MGMT status more often
\citep{zhang2020} and were rated more consistent by the clinicians and radiologists caring for these patients
\citep{gore2019}. Language models now draft such reports from automated measurements of brain tumours
\citep{valerio2025,herasrivera2026} and other lesions \citep{mansour2026}.

Reports written by language models can contain errors that a reader may not catch. AI-written chest radiograph reports
contained clinically significant errors \citep{tanno2025}, 6\% of oncology CT reports simplified by an open-weight
model contained factual errors \citep{prucker2025}, and systems that write text from tables misstate numbers
\citep{wiseman2017}. Automatic measures of factual accuracy track human judgement poorly \citep{gabriel2021}, so
checkers test each statement against the patient record \citep{zeba2026}, and errors can be planted in reports to
test such checkers \citep{rao2025rexerr}. Because patient records often cannot leave the hospital, the model should
also run locally, and open-weight models run on site can label radiology reports \citep{mukherjee2023} and extract
their findings as well as closed models \citep{nowak2025}.

We present PRISM (Progression Reading with Independent Scores and Measurements), a system for glioma follow-up MRI
that runs on one local computer (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). Unlike systems in which language-model agents read clinical
notes and assign a BT-RADS category \citep{jabal2026}, PRISM gives its language model the clinical history as data
and no decision to make, so every sentence it writes can be checked. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A local pipeline from MRI to a checked report.} PRISM outlines the tumour with a fixed public
segmentation network, a Swin UNETR trained on BraTS 2021 \citep{hatamizadeh2022} or the first-placed BraTS 2024
post-treatment model \citep{ferreira2024}, or takes the masks supplied with the dataset, and computes 27 measurements
of volume, shape, intensity and change. An open-weight language model (Qwen3.8-27B, served with vLLM
\citep{kwon2023}) writes the report from a structured record that holds every value with its source, and each
sentence lists the record entries it uses. No patient data leave the computer.
\item \textbf{Separate scores with stated uncertainty.} Three ridge logistic regressions \citep{pedregosa2011} score
each scan from the image measurements alone, with no dates, from the clinical history, and from the two combined. Each
score comes with the outcomes it cannot rule out, chosen to include the true outcome for 80\% of scans
\citep{angelopoulos2023}, a statement of whether the image and clinical-history scores agree, and the models'
performance on similar earlier scans.
\item \textbf{An evaluation of what each score adds.} We compare every score with a reference model of the time since
radiotherapy and the enhancing tumour volume, and split its AUC into pairs of scans from the same time window and from
different windows \citep{kallus2019}. This measures what the images add beyond what a clinician already knows.
\item \textbf{External validation without retraining.} Fitted once on 181 scans from 92 patients in MU-Glioma-Post, the
models were applied unchanged to 178 scans from 79 patients in LUMIERE and 69 scans from 69 patients in UCSF-ALPTDG, from the supplied masks and from PRISM's own
segmentation.
\item \textbf{A report checked against its record.} A deterministic checker and a verifier test every number and
direction in the report against the record. We test the checks themselves with 24 kinds of planted error, and all 406
reports written for held-out scans passed them.
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:2} describes the cohorts, PRISM's stages and the evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:3} reports the results
at the three centres, what the scores rest on, the effect of segmentation, and the conformal sets and report checks.
Section~\ref{sec:4} discusses what the results mean and their limits.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:2}

\subsection{Cohorts and label}\label{sec:2.1}

% Sources: TABLE1_COHORT.md; UCSF_DATA.md; UCSF_PLAN.md; DETECTION_TASK.md; INTENDED_USE.md; NUMBERS.md 2.2.
We used three public cohorts of patients followed with MRI after treatment for glioma. PRISM was developed on
MU-Glioma-Post \citep{mahmoud2025} and tested without retraining on LUMIERE \citep{suter2022} and UCSF-ALPTDG
\citep{fields2024} (Table~\ref{tab:1}). In all three, the primary population was grade 4 glioma.

The task was to distinguish the scan at which progression was documented from scans without progression. Each cohort
records progression differently, so the label follows each cohort's own data (Table~\ref{tab:eval}). At
MU-Glioma-Post and UCSF-ALPTDG, a scan without progression must be followed by at least 120 days without progression.
LUMIERE rates every scan, and UCSF-ALPTDG supplies two consecutive scans per patient, of which only the second is
labelled. Because the labels differ, models are compared only within a cohort.

At MU-Glioma-Post, the models were fitted on 181 labelled scans from 92 of the cohort's 126 grade 4 patients.
Performance was measured on the 146 of these scans (80 patients) taken after radiotherapy had started, the setting in
which PRISM is meant to be used. The external evaluation sets held 178 scans from 79 patients at LUMIERE and 69 scans
from 69 patients at UCSF-ALPTDG (Figure~\ref{fig:cohorts}). Because the 120-day rule leaves out scans with shorter
follow-up, we also evaluated every score at MU-Glioma-Post with all scans before progression counted as scans without
progression.

\paragraph*{Segmentation.} The tumour masks of each scan come from one of two fixed public segmentation networks,
which PRISM runs itself, or are supplied with the dataset (Table~\ref{tab:1}). The primary results use the supplied masks, so that every volume can be traced to a
public file. At MU-Glioma-Post and UCSF-ALPTDG, the supplied masks mark the enhancing tumour, necrosis, FLAIR
hyperintensity and the resection cavity. At LUMIERE, they are DeepBraTumIA masks with three labels, which count the
cavity as necrosis.

Both networks run with their public weights and settings and were not tuned on our cohorts. The first is the Swin
UNETR \citep{hatamizadeh2022} released for BraTS 2021 (fold 1; Figure~\ref{fig:1}b), a network of 62 million
parameters that marks the enhancing tumour, the tumour core and the whole tumour. It takes FLAIR, T1c, T1 and T2, each
scaled to zero mean and unit variance over the brain, in blocks of $128^3$ voxels that overlap by half. A voxel
belongs to a region when its probability exceeds 0.5. Necrosis is the core outside the enhancing tumour, and FLAIR
hyperintensity is the rest of the whole tumour. BraTS 2021 contains scans taken before surgery, so this network has no
resection-cavity label and supplies 22 of the 27 measurements. The second network is the winning BraTS 2024 model for
adult glioma after treatment \citep{deverdier2024,ferreira2024}, an ensemble of nnU-Net, MedNeXt and Swin UNETR
networks, run from the challenge organisers' container. It also marks the resection cavity and supplies all 27
measurements (Supplementary Section~S13.1).

\paragraph*{Measurements and clinical-history inputs.} From the masks and the T1c and FLAIR images, PRISM computes 27
measurements (Supplementary Table~S7). Nineteen describe the current scan: six log volumes and measures of composition,
shape, position and intensity. Eight describe change since the previous scan and since the patient's smallest volume.
Change uses earlier scans only and is zero at a patient's first scan. Five measurements need the cavity (Supplementary Section~S13.2).

PRISM computes the clinical-history inputs from the treatment course as of the scan day and from the patient's
characteristics, including IDH mutation and MGMT promoter methylation status (Table~\ref{tab:models}). PRISM also places each scan in a
time window after radiotherapy: before its end, within 12 weeks after its end, later, or unknown when dates are
missing. Each input enters only once it is known at the scan: the dose from
the start of radiotherapy, its duration and the time since its end after it has ended, and IDH and MGMT status from
the diagnosis date (Supplementary Section~S2).

\paragraph*{Three models.} The imaging, clinical-history and combined models are ridge logistic regressions on the
inputs listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}. Logistic regression turns a weighted sum of the inputs into a probability of
progression, and the ridge (L2) penalty pulls the weights towards zero, which keeps them stable with many correlated
inputs and few patients.

Each model is fitted with scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011}. Each missing input is set to its training median,
with an extra input marking that it was missing, and every input is standardised. The inverse penalty strength $C$ is
chosen from 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1 and 3 by the lowest mean log loss in threefold cross validation within the
training data, grouped by patient, and the model is refitted with that value on all training scans.

At LUMIERE and UCSF-ALPTDG, PRISM applies reduced models, fitted on MU with only the inputs that all three cohorts
record, because neither external centre records the full treatment course (Table~\ref{tab:1}; inputs in
Table~\ref{tab:models}). Both reduced models take a flag for scans in the first 12 weeks after radiotherapy. At MU the
flag is computed from the end of radiotherapy. At LUMIERE it is the rater's mark for scans taken less than three
months after radiotherapy. At UCSF-ALPTDG it marks scans 42 to 125 days after the start of radiotherapy, which assumes
a six-week course.

At UCSF-ALPTDG PRISM applies the full imaging model, with the one earlier scan in the dataset as the previous scan.
At LUMIERE, whose masks have three labels, the imaging model is fitted on MU with the three volumes and their change
since the previous scan. At the 10 LUMIERE scans with no previous scan, a model of the three current volumes is used
instead.

% Sources: prism/audit/conformal.py; METHODS_PLAN.md M2; RELIABILITY_CARD.md; REVISION_DATA.md R2; refs
% angelopoulos2023, vovk2022book, romano2020, barber2021limits, johansson2015, ding2023, mehrtens2023, mitchell2019.
\paragraph*{Confidence statements.} For each score, PRISM computes an 80\% split-conformal set
\citep{angelopoulos2023}, with its threshold taken from calibration patients that the model was not fitted on. Write $\hat p(1) = \hat p$ for a scan's score and $\hat p(0) = 1 - \hat p$. For each calibration scan $i$
with outcome $y_i$, the nonconformity score $s_i = 1 - \hat p_i(y_i)$ is the probability that the model did not give
to the true outcome. Each calibration scan is weighted by one over the number of its patient's calibration scans, so
every patient counts once. With $\alpha = 0.2$ and $n$ calibration patients, the threshold $\hat q$ is the smallest
$s_i$ at which the cumulative weight, taking the $s_i$ in increasing order, reaches $(1 - \alpha)(n + 1)$. A new scan
receives every outcome to which the model gave enough probability,
\begin{equation}
C(\hat p) = \{\, y \in \{0, 1\} : 1 - \hat p(y) \le \hat q \,\}.
\end{equation}
Supplementary Section~S13.3 gives the cases of an infinite threshold and of an empty set.

Each of the three models was refitted on 70\% of its training patients and calibrated on the other 30\%, as planned,
so that no calibration patient was used for fitting. At MU this was done inside each training fold of the cross
validation. For the external cohorts it was done once, on all MU training patients, and the resulting models and
thresholds were applied without retraining. The combined score's set is the primary one.

The 80\% holds on average over scans when new patients are exchangeable with the calibration patients, meaning that
swapping them would not change the joint distribution of their data; weighting by patient makes it approximate
\citep{romano2020,barber2021limits,mehrtens2023}.

% Sources: METHODS_PLAN.md M2; Supplementary Section S10.
We also computed group-conditional (Mondrian) sets, which compute $\hat q$ separately within each group of scans, so
that the 80\% target applies to each group as well as to the whole cohort \citep{vovk2022book}. A group combines the
time window after radiotherapy with the state of the enhancing tumour: absent (below 0.1\,cc), present with no
previous scan to compare, increased since the previous scan by at least 1\,cc and 40\%, or not increased. Groups with
fewer than 10 calibration patients fall back to a coarser level (\citealp{johansson2015,ding2023}; Supplementary Section~S10.2). Part of any gain in
coverage within a group can come from larger sets alone. To separate the two, we compared the group sets at MU with
planned sets at the $\alpha$ (0.175) that gives the same share of sets holding both outcomes (exploratory).

The agreement statement compares each of the clinical-history and imaging scores with the median of that model's
scores over the cohort's evaluated scans. The two models agree when both scores lie above their medians or both lie
below. The median uses no outcomes, and at MU it is taken in each cross-validation repeat.

The reliability card, modelled on model cards \citep{mitchell2019}, looks up the evaluated scans of each cohort that
fall in the same group as the scan, and counts those with and without progression. With at least 10 of each, the group
is in the tested tier, and the card also gives the three models' AUCs with 95\%
CIs from 1,000 bootstrap samples of patients. With fewer, the tier is few cases and the card gives only the counts.
With no scans, the tier is not tested. The record carries the tier of the scan's own cohort. The card fits no model;
it reads results already measured, including those for the scan it describes (Supplementary Section~S9). The agreement and tier rules were set after the first
results at the three cohorts had been seen (exploratory).

% Sources: prism/report/facts_v3_1.py; prism/report/prompts_v3_1.py; prism/report/checker.py;
% prism/report/verifier.py; REPORT_V3.md; REPORT_V3_1.md; METHODS_RESULTS.md M3; CHECKER_MUTATION.md;
% REVISION_DATA.md R3/R4; refs kwon2023, herasrivera2026, weinberg2018, gore2019, zeba2026, wiseman2017, gabriel2021,
% rao2025rexerr.
\paragraph*{Record.} PRISM writes everything it computes for a scan into one structured record, a flat list of named
entries. Each entry has a key, such as \code{meas.enhancing\_cc}, and holds one value or one fixed sentence. The
entries give the time after radiotherapy, the volumes and their change since the previous scan, each model's score
with its cohort median and the inputs it is based on, the conformal sets, the agreement statement, the reliability
card and four flags. The flags mark disagreement between the two models, unknown IDH or MGMT status or an input outside
the 1st to 99th percentile of its training values, a card with few cases or none, and an 80\% set that holds both
outcomes. Numbers are rounded as the record is built (scores
to two decimals, volumes to one). Wording that can be fixed in advance, such as the tier, each conformal set, the
agreement, each change in volume and the reason for each flag, is stored as a sentence for the language model to
copy. The record holds no outcome label.

\paragraph*{Report.} A local open-weight language model, Qwen3.8-27B, writes the report's prose from the record alone.
It runs offline on one GPU of the computer that runs PRISM, served with the vLLM library \citep{kwon2023}, so the
record never leaves that computer. Decoding is greedy (the model always takes its most likely next word), so the report does not depend on random
sampling. The prompt asks for sentences in a fixed order, from the scan's timing to one sentence for each raised flag (Supplementary Section~S13.4). It tells the model to copy every number as digits and every fixed phrase exactly, and to add no diagnosis, response
category, advice or probability words such as ``likely''. The model sees no images and has no tools, and every number
it may write is already in the record. Its output is constrained to a JSON schema: a list of sentences, each with its
text, one of four report sections and the keys of the record entries it uses. The schema does not ensure that a cited
key exists or that the text agrees with it; the checks below test both.

Fixed code fills in the rest of the report page: the tables of volumes and scores, the conformal sets, the card, the
flags, the segmentation slices and a fixed paragraph of limitations. The language model only turns record entries into
prose, a division of work also used to write brain tumour reports from extracted image features
\citep{herasrivera2026}. The page carries the history, comparison and change elements of the BT-RADS follow-up
template \citep{weinberg2018}, whose structured reports were rated more consistent than free prose \citep{gore2019}.
It leaves out the template's category and advice, because PRISM makes no decision (Supplementary Section~S8). Because
the enhancing volume varies between scans taken close together (Section~\ref{sec:3.3}), the report calls all volumes
indicative.

\paragraph*{Checks.} Two rule-based programs, neither using a language model, then compare the report with its
record. They test whether the report agrees with the record, and do not test whether the record is clinically
correct. The first, the checker, rejects a report that cannot be parsed, cites a key that is not in the record, or
writes a number that matches no cited entry up to the rounding written. It also rejects a report that leaves out or
misstates required content, or uses words the prompt forbids or words of certainty (Supplementary Section~S13.5). Similar rule-based checks test generated text against its source record \citep{zeba2026},
and a learned extractor does so for text generated from tables \citep{wiseman2017}. The checker was fixed before any
evaluation report was generated, and a rejected report is not edited or written again.

The prompt was revised once, after the checker rejected all 178 LUMIERE reports in the first evaluation because one
prompt line asked for a number that was in no cited entry (Supplementary Section~S13.4). The revised prompt was developed on 58 reports for 20 MU, 10
LUMIERE and 10 UCSF-ALPTDG patients. The evaluation reports were then generated again for the other patients, with the
checker unchanged.

The checker accepts a number that equals a number in any cited entry, and it does not read direction words (Supplementary Section~S13.5). The second program, the verifier, runs the
checker and adds two rules that tie each number and direction word to what it describes. It splits each sentence into clauses and assigns
each number and direction word to the nearest tumour region or score named in its clause. Each such number must equal,
up to its written rounding, a value that the cited entries give for that region or score. Each direction word on a
fixed list (larger, increased, grew, rose, higher, and their opposites) must agree with the direction that the cited
entries give for that region. The rules were revised once (Table~\ref{tab:2}) and then run once on the 276 MU and
LUMIERE evaluation reports (Supplementary Section~S10.3).

To measure what the two programs catch, we planted one error at a time in correct reports, using 24 kinds of error
(Supplementary Section~S10). Planted errors are also used to evaluate other factuality checks
\citep{gabriel2021,rao2025rexerr}. The correct reports came from test code (52 synthetic records), the language model (the 276 MU and
LUMIERE evaluation records) and a fixed template applied to the same records (Supplementary Section~S10.3). Three kinds of
error were held out while the rules were written: two that place a value under the wrong region or score, and one that
reverses the direction of a change. Because we wrote both the rules and these errors, a separate language model that
had not seen the rules also rewrote the synthetic reports with errors in its own words, with correct paraphrases as
controls (exploratory).

To test whether a report follows its record, we changed one entry in 30 evaluation records, six each for the card's
tier, the agreement, the change in enhancing volume, the out-of-range flag and the conformal set. The plan required at
least 27 reports to state the new value and not the old one. 



\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:2.3}

\paragraph*{Development and external testing.} On MU, every model was evaluated by five repeats of fivefold cross
validation grouped by patient: in each repeat, a model was fitted on four folds of patients and scored the scans of the
fifth. The AUC was computed over the held-out scores of the 146 evaluation scans (Section~\ref{sec:2.1}) and averaged
over the five repeats, because a single split of a small cohort gives an estimate with a wide error
\citep{varoquaux2018}. Each model was then fitted once on all 181 MU training scans and applied to LUMIERE and
UCSF-ALPTDG without retraining or recalibration (Supplementary Section~S14.1).

\paragraph*{Time-and-enhancement model.} As a reference for what a clinician reading the scan already knows
\citep{beaulieujones2021}, a ridge logistic regression was fitted on the time window after radiotherapy, the log
enhancing volume and its log ratio to the previous scan, in the same folds as PRISM's models. Externally it takes the
12-week flag (Section~\ref{sec:2.2}) and a flag for whether a previous scan exists. At UCSF-ALPTDG, where the flag is
approximated, it was used only in the segmentation comparison. Each score is compared with this model by the paired
difference in AUC. Scans were also ranked by timing alone, by the days since the end of radiotherapy and by the visit
number (exploratory; Table~\ref{tab:models}; Supplementary Section~S14.2).

\paragraph*{Splitting the AUC by time window.} The AUC is the share of pairs, one scan with progression and one
without, that a score ranks correctly. We split these pairs into pairs from the same time window, which the window
cannot separate, and pairs from different windows, which the window alone can often order. With $A_W$ and $A_B$ the
AUC over the pairs within and across windows, and $w$ the share of pairs within a window,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC} = w\, A_W + (1 - w)\, A_B .
\end{equation}
This is the decomposition of Kallus and Zhou \citep{kallus2019} with the pairs grouped in two, and $A_W$ is a
pair-weighted analogue of the covariate-adjusted AUC \citep{janes2008,janes2009}. The window is the time window at MU
and the 12-week flag externally. A window-only score, the share of training scans with progression in the scan's
window, shows how much of $A_B$ the window alone supplies (Supplementary Section~S10.1).


\paragraph*{Published radiomics pipeline.} We ran the radiomics pipeline published on MU-Glioma-Post
\citep{christodoulou2026} with its authors' public code and settings, on the same patients, masks, label and folds as
PRISM, and compared it with PRISM's imaging model. The pipeline computes 4,892 radiomic features of the current scan
with PyRadiomics \citep{vangriethuysen2017}, keeps the 256 best ranked within each training set, and fits a gradient
boosted tree model (LightGBM; Supplementary Section~S14.3).

The authors' code labels every scan of a patient who ever progressed as progression. We kept PRISM's label, so the
comparison is between two methods on one task and does not replicate the published estimate. The plan, fixed before
the run, required the pipeline to rank below the imaging model for that comparison to be reported as a difference.

\paragraph*{Segmentation comparison.} Three planned analyses replaced the supplied masks with a segmenter's and
refitted the models in the same folds: Swin UNETR at MU, with the supplied cavity kept; Swin UNETR alone at all three
centres (22 measurements), fitted on MU and applied without retraining; and the BraTS 2024 model in the same way. The
published pipeline was also run on the Swin UNETR masks at MU. Each score is compared with its value on the supplied
masks by the paired difference in AUC, under the rules in Table~\ref{tab:2} (Supplementary Section~S14.4).
Agreement with the supplied masks is measured by the Dice overlap, and the noise of the enhancing volume by its median
fold change between consecutive scans at most 30 days apart, at MU and LUMIERE.

\paragraph*{Tests fixed in advance.} Table~\ref{tab:2} lists each planned test, its rule and its outcome. The primary
results are the external AUCs, the gain over the time-and-enhancement model and the comparison with the published
pipeline. The other tests answer separate questions, each with its own rule, and no correction was made for multiple
testing. Analyses not fixed in advance are marked exploratory.



\paragraph*{Statistics.} Every AUC has a 95\% confidence interval (CI) from 1,000 bootstrap samples of patients
(2,000 in later analyses; Supplementary Section~S3). Each sample draws patients with replacement, keeps all their scans, and resamples the
held-out predictions without refitting the models. At MU each sample averages the AUC over the five repeats of cross
validation. Differences between scores are computed within the same samples. A difference whose CI lies above zero
counts as a gain, and one whose CI includes zero as no clear difference. No P values are reported. Calibration is
summarised by the calibration intercept and slope (\citealp{vancalster2019}; Supplementary Section~S11).

\subsection{Certainty and reports}\label{sec:3.4}

% Sources: NUMBERS.md 6 and 12B; SYSTEM_RESULTS.md S1.
The combined score's 80\% sets contained the true outcome in 82\%, 87\% and 78\% of scans at MU, LUMIERE and
UCSF-ALPTDG, and named a single outcome in 75\%, 62\% and 64\% of scans. Coverage varied within subgroups
(exploratory). At UCSF-ALPTDG the sets covered 97\% of scans with progression and 65\% (51\% to 79\%) of scans
without progression. At MU the group of scans later than 12 weeks after radiotherapy with increased enhancement was
covered at 68\% (55\% to 81\%).

In the two least covered MU groups, sets calibrated within groups covered no more scans than marginal sets enlarged

group (Supplementary Section~S10.2). Group calibration held both outcomes in 31\% of MU sets, against 25\% of marginal sets, and left 4\% empty. At
UCSF-ALPTDG it did not raise coverage in the least covered group, later scans without increased enhancement (61\%
marginal, 54\% within groups).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:4}

% Sources: Sections 3.1--3.2; refs gomaa2025, tarek2026, booth2022, shi2025, an2021, varoquaux2018, christodoulou2026.
The separate scores showed that the ranking rested on the time since radiotherapy at MU-Glioma-Post and on the
enhancing tumour at LUMIERE and UCSF-ALPTDG (Section~\ref{sec:3.2}). At
MU-Glioma-Post the follow-up schedule ties the time of a scan to its label. The time since treatment was also the
strongest input in an earlier external comparison \citep{gomaa2025}, and the scan interval affected a second model in
this setting (Section~\ref{sec:1}; \citealp{tarek2026}). A clinician already knows the time since radiotherapy.
PRISM's separate clinical-history score shows how much of the combined score that time supplies.

On PRISM's label and folds, the published radiomics pipeline ranked scans below PRISM's 27 measurements and no better
than the enhancing volume (Section~\ref{sec:3.1}). Like most models proposed for this decision
\citep{booth2022,shi2025}, it scores the image alone. The spread of its AUC over random held-out sets matches the instability of single splits of small cohorts \citep{an2021,varoquaux2018}, and the
published estimate came from one such split \citep{christodoulou2026}.

% Sources: Section 3.3; refs hoebel2024, deverdier2024.
The combined score kept its ranking when Swin UNETR outlined the tumour, and the MU imaging score did not
(Section~\ref{sec:3.3}). The combined and time-and-enhancement models rest on the time window and the enhancing
volume, which ranks scans similarly whichever network outlines it. The imaging model also uses measurements of shape,
intensity and change that depend on where the outline falls. Outlines of the same glioma differ between experts
\citep{hoebel2024}, and scans after treatment are harder to segment than scans before surgery \citep{deverdier2024},
so an image measurement depends on its segmenter as well as on the tumour. The post-treatment BraTS 2024 model
left the MU imaging score closer to its value on the supplied masks than Swin UNETR did (Section~\ref{sec:3.3}).

% Sources: Section 2.3; refs beaulieujones2021, kallus2019, janes2008.
The comparison with the time-and-enhancement model \citep{beaulieujones2021} and the split of the AUC by time window
\citep{kallus2019,janes2008} need no new images, only the time of each scan relative to treatment. The segmentation
comparison needs only a second public network. The three cohorts used here record treatment dates only
in part (Table~\ref{tab:1}), which limited the first two analyses at LUMIERE and UCSF-ALPTDG.

% Sources: Introduction (jabal2026, herasrivera2026); Section 3.4; refs pham2026, mansour2026, mehrtens2023.
A system that chains language models over clinical notes to assign a BT-RADS category \citep{jabal2026} reads the
clinical history as text, and one that phrases automated measurements with a language model
\citep{herasrivera2026} reports the images alone. PRISM enters the history as data in the record, so every sentence of
its report can be checked against a value. Other rule-based gates for clinical text forbid numbers altogether
\citep{pham2026} or trace numbers to a measurement file without testing the check \citep{mansour2026}, whereas
PRISM's checks were tested with planted errors (Section~\ref{sec:3.4}).

The conformal sets held their average coverage but not coverage within every group (Section~\ref{sec:3.4}), as
expected when patients are exchangeable only across the whole cohort \citep{mehrtens2023}. With groups this small,
calibrating within them moved coverage between groups more than it raised it. A centre adopting PRISM would
calibrate the sets on its own scans.

% Sources: NUMBERS.md 10 (limits); Sections 2.1, 2.3; REPORT_V3_1.md Limits.
This study has limitations. PRISM was developed on one public cohort and tested at two external centres with few
evaluation scans, so the external intervals are wide. The label is progression as each centre documented it, without
pathological or consensus confirmation, and at LUMIERE and UCSF-ALPTDG it partly reflects the images the scores use.
The supplied masks and both segmenters give reference outlines that are not ground truth. At UCSF-ALPTDG the
radiotherapy window was approximated from its start, and the time-and-enhancement model was applied there only
outside the planned comparison. MU holds few scans with progression in the first 12 weeks after radiotherapy. No
cohort supplied perfusion or diffusion imaging, which have separated progression from treatment effects in other
studies \citep{taylor2022}. The checks test only whether a report agrees with its record, and no clinician has yet read
the reports. The next step is a reader study in which neuroradiologists judge the reports against the scans. Further
cohorts, such as Burdenko and UCSD-PTGBM, would fill the groups of scans that are now too small for the reliability
card and allow the conformal sets to be calibrated on scans from more than one centre. The verifier's list of
direction words should also be widened and tested on errors it has not seen.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

We built PRISM so that a reader can see, at each follow-up scan, what its score rests on. Keeping the scores separate
showed that the same models ranked scans mainly through the time since radiotherapy at the development centre and more
through the enhancing tumour at the external centres, a difference that a single AUC does not reveal. A PRISM report
places the imaging score beside the clinical-history score, states how certain each is, and lets every number in it be
traced to the scan's record.
