\begin{abstract}
Models for glioma follow-up MRI report high accuracy, but a single AUC cannot show what drives their predictions. We
built a new framework, PRISM (Progression Reading with Independent Scores and Measurements), running on one local
computer. It segments each scan with the winning BraTS 2024 post-treatment model, scores it from image measurements,
clinical history and both with three logistic regressions and conformal sets, and has a local language model write a
checked report of what each score rests on. On MU-Glioma-Post, the combined model reached an AUC of 0.82 in repeated
cross validation, and up to 1.00 (mean 0.84) on single held-out splits, the design of earlier studies. Unlike most
earlier models, PRISM was tested without retraining at two other centres, reaching 0.79 at LUMIERE and 0.81 at
UCSF-ALPTDG, against a pooled AUC of 0.81 for earlier models tested at another centre.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
glioma surveillance, brain tumour segmentation, large language models, conformal prediction, external validation
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

Gliomas make up about a quarter of all primary brain tumours, and glioblastoma alone about half of the malignant
ones \citep{ostrom2023}. Even with surgery, radiotherapy and temozolomide, median survival is about 15 months
\citep{stupp2005}. After treatment, patients are followed with MRI every few months, and each scan feeds a decision:
continue treatment, scan again sooner, or change treatment.

Reading these scans is hard because treatment can look like progression. Pseudoprogression follows chemoradiotherapy
in 20--30\% of patients \citep{wen2010}. It occurs more often when the MGMT promoter is methylated \citep{brandes2008}.
In one cohort, half of the patients whose first scan after radiotherapy looked progressive had pseudoprogression
\citep{taal2008}. Clinicians therefore read each scan against the time since treatment. RANO 2.0 asks for a repeat
scan or histology before progression is called within 12 weeks of radiotherapy \citep{wen2023}.

Advanced MRI helps in some studies but has not solved the problem. In a review of 30 small studies, 13 at high
risk of bias, diffusion and perfusion MRI reached pooled AUCs of 0.90--0.93 in the 25 that could be pooled
\citep{taylor2022}. Adding them improved a radiomics model developed in 61 patients and tested in 34 others
\citep{kim2019}. In 105 patients with suspected pseudoprogression, however, they did not significantly improve on
clinical data and MGMT status \citep{leone2023}.

Machine learning models for this decision are mostly built on the image alone and tested only within the cohort they
were developed on. Studies of treatment response have small samples and a high risk of bias \citep{booth2022}. Of 51
radiomics studies in neuro-oncology, nine in ten of them on glioma, 29\% were validated externally \citep{park2020}.
Of 20 studies of automated response assessment, 3 considered clinical characteristics \citep{shi2025}. Single splits
of small cohorts give unstable estimates \citep{an2021}. Most deep learning algorithms for radiological diagnosis lose
performance on external data \citep{yu2022}. In a meta-analysis of 31 studies, models tested at another institution
reached a pooled AUC of 0.81, against 0.91 at the institution that developed them \citep{zhang2026meta}. A radiomics
model published on MU-Glioma-Post, the cohort used in this study, was evaluated on a single held-out set of 30
patients (AUC 0.80) \citep{christodoulou2026}.

A single AUC does not show what drives a model's predictions. Models learn shortcuts: signals that predict the label
but are not what the reader cares about \citep{geirhos2020,zech2018,badgeley2019}. In this setting, a model of
clinical data alone matched one using MRI on an external test set \citep{gomaa2025}. The interval between scans
affected a model built on the RANO rules \citep{tarek2026}. The useful measure is what a model adds beyond what a
clinician already knows \citep{pencina2008,beaulieujones2021}. Saliency maps drawn after the fact localise
abnormalities poorly and can be insensitive to the model's own weights \citep{arun2021,saporta2022}. A model that is
interpretable by design is therefore preferable \citep{rudin2019}.

The image measurements themselves depend on how the tumour is outlined. After surgery and radiotherapy the scan
contains a resection cavity and treatment changes, which make segmentation harder. The BraTS 2024 challenge was set
up for this setting \citep{deverdier2024}. Experts disagree about the quality of the same glioma segmentation, and
their own outlines of the same tumour overlap only in part \citep{hoebel2024}. Measured volumes therefore depend on
whose outlines are used.

A score is also more useful when it states its uncertainty. Conformal prediction turns a score into a set of possible
outcomes that contains the true one at a chosen rate \citep{angelopoulos2023}. The guarantee does not hold within
subgroups or under distribution shift \citep{mehrtens2023}.

Finally, the result reaches the clinician as a report, and reports vary. Of 220 European centres, 60\% assessed
follow-up scans qualitatively, 27\% applied RANO and 23\% used a reporting template \citep{thust2018}. After the
structured BT-RADS system was adopted \citep{weinberg2018}, reports mentioned bevacizumab and MGMT status more often
\citep{zhang2020}. They were also rated more consistent by the clinicians and radiologists caring for these patients
\citep{gore2019}. Language models now draft such reports from automated measurements of brain tumours
\citep{valerio2025,herasrivera2026} and other lesions \citep{mansour2026}.

Reports written by language models can contain errors that a reader may not catch. AI-written chest radiograph reports
contained clinically significant errors \citep{tanno2025}. Of oncology CT reports simplified by an open-weight model,
6\% contained factual errors \citep{prucker2025}. Neural systems that write text from tables misstate many of the
numbers they report \citep{wiseman2017}. Automatic measures of factual accuracy track human judgement poorly
\citep{gabriel2021}, so checkers test each statement against the patient record \citep{zeba2026}. Errors can be
planted in radiology reports to build test sets for methods that correct them \citep{rao2025rexerr}. Patient records
often cannot leave the hospital, so the model should also run locally. Open-weight models run on site can label
radiology reports \citep{mukherjee2023} and extract their findings comparably to closed models \citep{nowak2025}.

We present PRISM (Progression Reading with Independent Scores and Measurements), a new system for glioma follow-up MRI
that runs on one local computer. PRISM works from conventional MRI and the clinical record, both part of every
routine follow-up. In another recent system, a language-model agent extracts clinical variables from the notes, and
rules combine them with automated tumour volumes to assign a BT-RADS category \citep{jabal2026}. PRISM's language
model makes no decision. It writes up values computed elsewhere, so every sentence it writes can be checked. Our
contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A new local system is developed. It segments the tumour on each scan with the top-ranked model of the BraTS 2024
post-treatment challenge \citep{ferreira2024}, used without fine-tuning. We compare this segmenter with Swin UNETR
trained on BraTS 2021 \citep{hatamizadeh2022} and with the masks supplied with each dataset. From each segmentation,
PRISM derives 22 measurements of tumour volume, shape, intensity and change from earlier scans. No patient data leave
the computer.
\item PRISM separates imaging and clinical-history scores, each reported with its uncertainty. Three ridge logistic
regressions \citep{lecessie1992} score each scan: from the image measurements alone, which contain no dates; from the
clinical history; and from the two combined. Each score comes with the set of outcomes it cannot exclude
\citep{angelopoulos2023}, calibrated to contain the true outcome in 80\% of scans. It also comes with a statement of
whether the imaging and clinical-history scores agree, and with the models' performance on comparable earlier scans.
\item PRISM provides an evaluation of what each score adds to what the clinician already knows. We compare each score with a reference
model based on the time since radiotherapy and the enhancing tumour volume. We also measure the contribution of each
group of inputs to the score, as its share of each scan's score and as the fall in AUC when it is left out. At
MU-Glioma-Post, the combined model reached an AUC of 0.82 (0.76--0.89) in repeated cross validation, and up to 1.00
(mean 0.84) on single held-out splits of 18 patients, the design of earlier studies on this cohort
\citep{christodoulou2026}.
\item External validation is performed without retraining. We fitted the models once, on 181 scans from 92 patients in
MU-Glioma-Post, and applied them unchanged to 178 scans from 79 patients in LUMIERE and 69 scans from 69 patients in
UCSF-ALPTDG. These scores used PRISM's segmenter, with the supplied masks as a reference. The combined model reached
0.79 (0.72--0.86) at LUMIERE and 0.81 (0.70--0.90) at UCSF-ALPTDG, against a pooled AUC of 0.81 for earlier models
tested at another institution \citep{zhang2026meta}.
\item Report is generated with deterministic verification. The language model, Qwen3.8-27B served with vLLM
\citep{kwon2023}, writes the report only from a structured record that holds each value with its source. A rule-based
checker and verifier test every number and direction against that record. All reports for held-out scans passed the
checker, and both programs were tested with 24 types of planted error.
\end{enumerate}

evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:3} reports the results at the three centres, what the scores rest on, the effect of

\section{Methods}\label{sec:2}

Figure~\ref{fig:1} gives an overview of this section. PRISM reads each follow-up MRI scan of a patient treated for glioma (Figure~\ref{fig:1}a) and returns three scores of progression with a written report. \subsection{Cohorts and label}\label{sec:2.1}

% Sources: TABLE1_COHORT.md; UCSF_DATA.md; UCSF_PLAN.md; DETECTION_TASK.md; INTENDED_USE.md; NUMBERS.md 2.2.
We used three public cohorts of patients followed with MRI after treatment for glioma. PRISM is developed on
MU-Glioma-Post \citep{mahmoud2025,yaseen2025tcia} and tested without retraining on LUMIERE \citep{suter2022} and UCSF-ALPTDG
\citep{fields2024} (Table~\ref{tab:1}). In all three, the primary population was grade 4 glioma.

The task was to distinguish the scan at which progression was documented from scans without progression. Each cohort
records progression differently, so the label follows each cohort's own data (Table~\ref{tab:eval}). At
MU-Glioma-Post and UCSF-ALPTDG, a scan without progression must be followed by at least 120 days without progression.
LUMIERE rates every scan, and UCSF-ALPTDG supplies two consecutive scans per patient, of which only the second is
labelled. Because the labels differ, models are compared only within a cohort.

At MU-Glioma-Post, the models were fitted on 181 labelled scans from 92 of the cohort's 126 grade 4 patients.
Performance was measured on the 146 of these scans (80 patients) taken after radiotherapy had started. This is the setting
in which PRISM is meant to be used. The external evaluation sets held 178 scans from 79 patients at LUMIERE and 69 scans
from 69 patients at UCSF-ALPTDG (Figure~\ref{fig:cohorts}). The 120-day rule leaves out scans with shorter follow-up.
We therefore also evaluated every score at MU-Glioma-Post with all scans before progression counted as scans without
progression.

\paragraph*{Segmentation.} PRISM segments each scan with the winning model \citep{ferreira2024} of the BraTS 2024
challenge for adult glioma after treatment \citep{deverdier2024} (Figure~\ref{fig:1}b). This model is an ensemble of nnU-Net
\citep{isensee2021}, MedNeXt \citep{roy2023} and Swin UNETR networks, run with the BraTS orchestrator
\citep{kofler2025}. It marks the enhancing tumour, necrosis, FLAIR hyperintensity and the resection cavity. The primary
results use its outlines at all three centres, without the five measurements that need the cavity (Section~\ref{sec:3.3}). This
choice is post hoc. It was made after a planned comparison had shown that the cavity measurements lowered the results
with these outlines. The challenge's training data include scans from UCSF and may include scans from MU,
but the network learned outlines only, with no progression label.

The masks supplied with each dataset are the reference (Table~\ref{tab:1}). At MU-Glioma-Post and UCSF-ALPTDG, they
mark the same four regions. At LUMIERE, they are DeepBraTumIA masks with three labels \citep{suter2022}, which count
the cavity as necrosis.

PRISM runs both of its networks with their public weights and settings, and neither was tuned on our cohorts. The
second network is compared as an alternative segmentation stage. It is the Swin UNETR \citep{hatamizadeh2022}
released for BraTS 2021 (fold 1), a network of 62 million parameters. It marks the enhancing tumour, the tumour core
and the whole tumour. It takes FLAIR, T1c, T1 and T2, each
scaled to zero mean and unit variance over the brain, in blocks of $128^3$ voxels that overlap by half. A voxel
belongs to a region when its probability exceeds 0.5. Necrosis is the core outside the enhancing tumour, and FLAIR
hyperintensity is the rest of the whole tumour. BraTS 2021 contains scans taken before surgery \citep{baid2021}. This network therefore has
no resection-cavity label and supplies 22 of the 27 measurements.

\paragraph*{Measurements.} From the masks and the T1c and FLAIR images, PRISM computes 27
measurements. Nineteen describe the current scan: six log volumes and measures of composition,
shape, position and intensity. Eight describe change since the previous scan and since the patient's smallest volume.
Change uses earlier scans only and is zero at a patient's first scan. Five measurements need the cavity. The
primary results therefore use the other 22. With the supplied masks, all 27 are used.

PRISM computes the clinical-history inputs from the treatment course as of the scan day and from the patient's
characteristics, including IDH mutation and MGMT promoter methylation status (Table~\ref{tab:models}). PRISM also places each scan in a
time window after radiotherapy: before its end, within 12 weeks after its end, later, or unknown when dates are
missing. Each input enters only once it is known at the scan. The dose enters
from the start of radiotherapy. Its duration and the time since its end enter after it has ended. IDH and MGMT status
enter from the diagnosis date.

\paragraph*{Models.} The imaging, clinical-history and combined models are ridge logistic regressions \citep{lecessie1992} on the
inputs listed in Table~\ref{tab:models}. Logistic regression turns a weighted sum of the inputs into a probability of
progression. The ridge (L2) penalty pulls the weights towards zero. This keeps them stable when there are many
correlated inputs and few patients.

Each model is fitted with scikit-learn \citep{pedregosa2011}. Each missing input is set to its training median,
with an extra input marking that it was missing, and every input is standardised. The inverse penalty strength $C$ is
chosen from 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1 and 3. The chosen value gives the lowest mean log loss in threefold cross
validation within the training data, grouped by patient. The model is then refitted with that value on all training
scans.

Neither external centre records the full treatment course (Table~\ref{tab:1}). At LUMIERE and UCSF-ALPTDG, PRISM
therefore applies reduced models, fitted on MU with only the inputs that all three cohorts record (inputs in
Table~\ref{tab:models}). Both reduced models take a flag for scans in the first 12 weeks after radiotherapy. At MU the
flag is computed from the end of radiotherapy. At LUMIERE it is the rater's mark for scans taken less than three
months after radiotherapy. At UCSF-ALPTDG it marks scans 42 to 125 days after the start of radiotherapy, which assumes
a six-week course.

With PRISM's segmenter, the full imaging model is applied at both external centres, and the reduced combined model
takes its 22 measurements. At UCSF-ALPTDG the one earlier scan in the dataset is the previous scan. With the supplied
masks, UCSF-ALPTDG uses the full imaging model as well. At LUMIERE, the supplied masks have three labels. With
these masks, the imaging model is fitted on MU with the three volumes and their change since the previous scan. At the
10 LUMIERE scans with no previous scan, a model of the three current volumes is used instead.

% Sources: prism/audit/conformal.py; METHODS_PLAN.md M2; RELIABILITY_CARD.md; REVISION_DATA.md R2; refs
% angelopoulos2023, vovk2022book, romano2020, barber2021limits, johansson2015, ding2023, mehrtens2023, mitchell2019.
\paragraph*{Evidence.} For each score, PRISM computes an 80\% split-conformal set
\citep{angelopoulos2023}. Its threshold is taken from calibration patients that the model was not fitted on. Write $\hat p(1) = \hat p$ for a scan's score and $\hat p(0) = 1 - \hat p$. For each calibration scan $i$
with outcome $y_i$, the nonconformity score $s_i = 1 - \hat p_i(y_i)$ is the probability that the model did not give
to the true outcome. Each calibration scan is weighted by one over the number of its patient's calibration scans, so
every patient counts once. Take $\alpha = 0.2$ and $n$ calibration patients, and sort the $s_i$ in increasing
order. The threshold $\hat q$ is the smallest $s_i$ at which the cumulative weight reaches $(1 - \alpha)(n + 1)$. A new scan
receives every outcome to which the model gave enough probability,
\begin{equation}
C(\hat p) = \{\, y \in \{0, 1\} : 1 - \hat p(y) \le \hat q \,\}.
\end{equation}

Each of the three models was refitted on 70\% of its training patients and calibrated on the other 30\%, as planned,
so that no calibration patient was used for fitting. At MU this was done inside each training fold of the cross
validation. For the external cohorts it was done once, on all MU training patients, and the resulting models and
thresholds were applied without retraining. The combined score's set is the primary one.

The 80\% holds on average over scans when new patients are exchangeable with the calibration patients, meaning that
swapping them would not change the joint distribution of their data \citep{romano2020,barber2021limits,mehrtens2023}.
Because patients contribute different numbers of scans, weighting by patient makes the guarantee approximate.

% Sources: METHODS_PLAN.md M2; Supplementary Section S10.
We also computed group-conditional (Mondrian) sets. These compute $\hat q$ separately within each group of scans, so
that the 80\% target applies to each group as well as to the whole cohort \citep{vovk2022book}. A group combines the
time window after radiotherapy with the state of the enhancing tumour: absent (below 0.1\,cc), present with no
previous scan to compare, increased since the previous scan by at least 1\,cc and 40\%, or not increased. Small calibration
groups make the thresholds conservative \citep{johansson2015}. Groups with fewer than 10 calibration patients therefore
fall back to a coarser level \citep{ding2023}. Part of any gain in
coverage within a group can come from larger sets alone. To separate the two, we compared the group sets at MU with
planned sets at the $\alpha$ (0.155) that gives the same share of sets holding both outcomes (exploratory).

The agreement statement compares each of the clinical-history and imaging scores with the median of that model's
scores over the cohort's evaluated scans. The two models agree when both scores lie above their medians or both lie
below. The median uses no outcomes, and at MU it is taken in each cross-validation repeat.

The reliability card is modelled on model cards \citep{mitchell2019}. It looks up the evaluated scans of each cohort
that fall in the same group as the scan, and counts those with and without progression. With at least 10 of each, the
group is in the tested tier. The card then also gives the three models' AUCs with 95\% CIs from 1,000 bootstrap
samples of patients. With fewer, the tier is few cases and the card gives only the counts.
With no scans, the tier is not tested. The record carries the tier of the scan's own cohort. The card fits no model.
It reads results already measured, including those for the scan it describes. The agreement and tier rules were set after the first
results at the three cohorts had been seen (exploratory).

% Sources: prism/report/facts_v3_1.py; prism/report/prompts_v3_1.py; prism/report/checker.py;
% prism/report/verifier.py; REPORT_V3.md; REPORT_V3_1.md; METHODS_RESULTS.md M3; CHECKER_MUTATION.md;
% REVISION_DATA.md R3/R4; refs kwon2023, herasrivera2026, weinberg2018, gore2019, zeba2026, wiseman2017, gabriel2021,
% rao2025rexerr.
\paragraph*{Record.} PRISM writes everything it computes for a scan into one structured record, a flat list of named
entries. Each entry has a key, such as \code{meas.enhancing\_cc}, and holds one value or one fixed sentence. The
entries give the time after radiotherapy, the volumes and their change since the previous scan, each model's score
with its cohort median and the inputs it is based on, the conformal sets, the agreement statement, the reliability
card and four flags. The flags mark disagreement between the two models, unknown IDH or MGMT status or an input outside
the 1st to 99th percentile of its training values, a card with few cases or none, and an 80\% set that holds both
outcomes. Numbers are rounded as the record is built (scores
to two decimals, volumes to one). Wording that can be fixed in advance, such as the tier, each conformal set, the
agreement, each change in volume and the reason for each flag, is stored as a sentence for the language model to
copy. The record holds no outcome label.

\paragraph*{Report and checks.} A local open-weight language model, Qwen3.8-27B, writes the report's prose from the record alone.
It runs offline on one GPU of the computer that runs PRISM, served with the vLLM library \citep{kwon2023}, so the
record never leaves that computer. Decoding is greedy (the model always takes its most likely next word), so the report does not depend on random
sampling. The prompt asks for sentences in a fixed order, from the scan's timing to one sentence for each raised flag. It tells the model to copy every number as digits and every fixed phrase exactly, and to add no diagnosis, response
category, advice or probability words such as ``likely''. The model sees no images and has no tools, and every number
it may write is already in the record. Its output is constrained to a JSON schema: a list of sentences, each with its
text, one of four report sections and the keys of the record entries it uses. The schema does not ensure that a cited
key exists or that the text agrees with it. The checks below test both.

Fixed code fills in the rest of the report page: the tables of volumes and scores, the conformal sets, the card, the
flags, the segmentation slices and a fixed paragraph of limitations. The language model only turns record entries into
prose. This division of work is also used to write brain tumour reports from extracted image features
\citep{herasrivera2026}. The page carries the history, comparison and change elements of the BT-RADS follow-up
template \citep{weinberg2018}, whose adoption was followed by higher ratings of report consistency \citep{gore2019}.
It leaves out the template's category and advice, because PRISM makes no decision. Because
the enhancing volume varies between scans taken close together (Section~\ref{sec:3.3}), the report calls all volumes
indicative.

Two rule-based programs then compare the report with its record. Neither uses a language
model. They test whether the report agrees with the record. They do not test whether the record is clinically
correct. The first, the checker, rejects a report that cannot be parsed, cites a key that is not in the record, or
writes a number that matches no cited entry up to the rounding written. It also rejects a report that leaves out or
misstates required content, or uses words the prompt forbids or words of certainty. Similar rule-based checks test generated text against its source record \citep{zeba2026}.
A learned extractor does so for text generated from tables \citep{wiseman2017}. The checker was fixed before any
evaluation report was generated. A rejected report is not edited or written again.

The prompt was revised once, after the checker rejected all 178 LUMIERE reports in the first evaluation because one
prompt line asked for a number that was in no cited entry. The revised prompt was developed on 58 reports for 20 MU, 10
LUMIERE and 10 UCSF-ALPTDG patients. The evaluation reports were then generated again for the other patients, with the
checker unchanged.

The checker accepts a number that equals a number in any cited entry. It does not read direction words. The second program, the verifier, runs the
checker and adds two rules that tie each number and direction word to what it describes. It splits each sentence into clauses and assigns
each number and direction word to the nearest tumour region or score named in its clause. Each such number must equal,
up to its written rounding, a value that the cited entries give for that region or score. Each direction word on a
fixed list (larger, increased, grew, rose, higher, and their opposites) must agree with the direction that the cited
entries give for that region. The rules were revised once (Table~\ref{tab:2}) and then run once on the 276 MU and
LUMIERE evaluation reports from the supplied masks.

To measure what the two programs catch, we planted one error at a time in correct reports, using 24 kinds of error. Planted errors are also used to evaluate other factuality checks
\citep{gabriel2021}. The correct reports came from test code (52 synthetic records), the language model (the 276 MU and
LUMIERE evaluation records from the supplied masks) and a fixed template applied to the same records. Three kinds of
error were held out while the rules were written: two that place a value under the wrong region or score, and one that
reverses the direction of a change. We wrote both the rules and these errors. A separate language model that
had not seen the rules therefore also rewrote the synthetic reports with errors in its own words, with correct paraphrases as
controls (exploratory).

To test whether a report follows its record, we changed one entry in 30 evaluation records, six each for the card's
tier, the agreement, the change in enhancing volume, the out-of-range flag and the conformal set. The plan required at
least 27 reports to state the new value and not the old one. 



\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:2.3}

\paragraph*{Development and external testing.} On MU, every model was evaluated by five repeats of fivefold cross
validation grouped by patient. In each repeat, a model was fitted on four folds of patients and scored the scans of the
fifth. The AUC was computed over the held-out scores of the 146 evaluation scans (Section~\ref{sec:2.1}) and averaged
over the five repeats, because a single split of a small cohort gives an estimate with a wide error
\citep{an2021,varoquaux2018}. Each model was then fitted once on all 181 MU training scans and applied to LUMIERE and
UCSF-ALPTDG without retraining or recalibration. For comparison with studies evaluated on
one held-out split \citep{christodoulou2026}, the combined model was also fitted and scored on 100 random held-out sets
of 18 of the 92 grade 4 patients, drawn as in an earlier re-implementation of that study (exploratory).

\paragraph*{Time-and-enhancement model.} A ridge logistic regression was fitted on the time window after radiotherapy,
the log enhancing volume and its log ratio to the previous scan, in the same folds as PRISM's models. It serves as a
reference for what a clinician reading the scan already knows, in line with benchmarking models against information
clinicians already hold \citep{beaulieujones2021}. Externally it takes the
12-week flag (Section~\ref{sec:2.2}) and a flag for whether a previous scan exists. At UCSF-ALPTDG, where the flag is
approximated, it was used only in the segmentation comparison. Each score is compared with this model by the paired
difference in AUC. Scans were also ranked by timing alone, by the days since the end of radiotherapy and by the visit
number (exploratory; Table~\ref{tab:models}).

\paragraph*{Contribution of each group of inputs.} To show what each score rests on, the inputs were divided into
six groups: the patient's characteristics; the treatment course and dates; the volumes; composition, shape and
position; intensity; and change since earlier scans (Table~\ref{tab:models}). Each group
was left out in turn, the model was refitted in the same folds, and its AUC was compared with the full model's in the
same bootstrap samples. Each model is a weighted sum of standardised inputs, so the linear part of each scan's score
also splits into one part per group. A group's part is the sum of weight times value over the group's inputs and their
missing-value indicators. A group's share of the score is the size of its part over the sum of the sizes of all parts
(exploratory).


\paragraph*{Published radiomics model.} We ran the radiomics model published on MU-Glioma-Post
\citep{christodoulou2026} with its authors' public code and settings, on the same patients, masks, label and folds as
PRISM. We compared it with PRISM's imaging model. It computes 4,892 radiomic features of the current scan
with PyRadiomics \citep{vangriethuysen2017}, keeps the 256 best ranked within each training set, and fits a gradient
boosted tree model (LightGBM, \citealp{ke2017}).

The authors' code labels every scan of a patient who ever progressed as progression. We kept PRISM's label, so the
comparison is between two methods on one task and does not replicate the published estimate. The plan, fixed before
the run, required the radiomics model to rank below the imaging model for that comparison to be reported as a difference.

\paragraph*{Segmentation comparison.} Three planned analyses replaced the supplied masks with a segmenter's and
refitted the models in the same folds. The first used Swin UNETR at MU, with the supplied cavity kept. The second used
Swin UNETR alone at all three centres (22 measurements), fitted on MU and applied without retraining. The third used
the BraTS 2024 model in the same way, and this model then became PRISM's segmentation stage (Section~\ref{sec:2.2}).
The published radiomics model was also run on the Swin UNETR masks at MU. Each score is compared with its value on the
supplied masks by the paired difference in AUC, under the rules in Table~\ref{tab:2}. Agreement with the supplied masks
is measured by the Dice overlap \citep{dice1945}. At MU and LUMIERE, the noise of the enhancing volume is measured by
its median fold change between consecutive scans at most 30 days apart.

\paragraph*{Tests fixed in advance.} Table~\ref{tab:2} lists each planned test, its rule and its outcome. The primary
results are the external AUCs, the gain over the time-and-enhancement model and the comparison with the published
radiomics model. The other tests answer separate questions, each with its own rule. No correction was made for multiple
testing. Analyses not fixed in advance are marked exploratory.



\paragraph*{Statistics.} Every AUC has a 95\% confidence interval (CI) from 1,000 bootstrap samples of patients
\citep{field2007} (2,000 in later analyses). Each sample draws patients with replacement, keeps all their scans, and resamples the
held-out predictions without refitting the models. At MU each sample averages the AUC over the five repeats of cross
validation. Differences between scores are computed within the same samples. A difference whose CI lies above zero
counts as a gain, and one whose CI includes zero as no clear difference. No P values are reported. Calibration is
summarised by the calibration intercept and slope \citep{vancalster2019}.

\subsection{The combined score's sets held their coverage}\label{sec:3.4}

The combined score's 80\% sets contained the true outcome in 82\%, 82\% and 80\% of scans at MU, LUMIERE and

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:4}

PRISM ranked follow-up scans better than the radiomics model of \citet{christodoulou2026}, previously published
on the same cohort. On the same patients, masks, label and folds, PRISM's imaging model reached an AUC of 0.71
against 0.58 for that model, and its clinical-history model reached 0.82 (Section~\ref{sec:3.1}). PRISM's models also
kept their ranking at two hospitals whose scans they had never seen, without retraining. Most models in this field are
built on the image alone \citep{booth2022,shi2025} and tested only within the cohort they were developed on
\citep{booth2022,park2020,shi2025}.
A single split of a small cohort gives unstable estimates \citep{an2021,varoquaux2018}. An earlier re-implementation of
that model ranged from 0.32 to 0.88 over random held-out sets. PRISM's combined model reached up to 1.00 (mean 0.84) on
sets drawn the same way (Section~\ref{sec:3.1}). PRISM was evaluated by repeated cross validation and at two external
centres instead.

But a good AUC does not say why a model ranks scans well, so PRISM breaks the score down. The imaging and
clinical-history scores are computed separately, and each score splits into the parts that come from each group of
inputs. The reader can therefore see what the score rests on. At MU-Glioma-Post the treatment course and dates made up
a median of 44\% of the combined score, the largest single group, and the image inputs together 43\%
(Section~\ref{sec:3.2}). Earlier studies also found that a model of clinical data alone can match one using MRI
\citep{gomaa2025}, and that the interval between scans affects models in this setting \citep{tarek2026}. A clinician
already knows when a scan was taken. PRISM shows how much of its score that knowledge supplies and how much the images
add.

These two checks can be applied to other models in this field. Comparing a score with a simple model of what the
clinician already knows \citep{beaulieujones2021}, here the time since radiotherapy and the enhancing tumour, needs only
the date of each scan relative to treatment. The share of the score from each group of inputs needs only the model's
weights. We suggest that studies of progression models report both beside the AUC.

PRISM's combined score kept its ranking when PRISM's segmenter replaced the supplied masks. The imaging score depended
more on the outlines (Section~\ref{sec:3.3}). The combined score rests largely on the treatment dates, which no
segmenter changes, and on the enhancing volume, which changed little between segmenters at MU but more at LUMIERE. The
imaging score also uses the shape and intensity of the outline. Outlines of the same tumour differ between experts
\citep{hoebel2024} and are harder to draw after treatment \citep{deverdier2024}. The BraTS 2024 model, trained on
scans after treatment, kept the imaging score closer to its value on the supplied masks than the older Swin UNETR did,
so PRISM runs end to end from the MRI.

PRISM's reports can be checked line by line. Other systems use language models to extract clinical variables from
notes, which rules then combine with tumour volumes to assign a BT-RADS category \citep{jabal2026}. Others use them to
write reports from features of the images alone \citep{herasrivera2026}. In PRISM, the clinical history is stored as
data in the record. The language model only writes from that record, and every number it writes is traced back to its
source. Other checks for clinical text either forbid numbers \citep{pham2026} or trace them without reporting a test
of the check itself \citep{mansour2026}. PRISM's checks were tested by planting errors in correct reports
(Section~\ref{sec:3.5}).

The combined score's 80\% sets held their target across each cohort, but not in every small group of scans, such as
later scans with increased enhancement (Section~\ref{sec:3.4}). This is expected, because the guarantee holds on
average over scans rather than within each group \citep{mehrtens2023}. A hospital using PRISM could recalibrate the sets
on its own scans.

This study has limitations. PRISM was developed on one public cohort, and the two external cohorts are small, so their
intervals are wide. Progression is taken as each centre recorded it, without biopsy or panel confirmation. The
supplied masks and the segmenters' outlines are references, not ground truth. PRISM's segmentation stage and its 22
measurements were chosen after the planned segmentation comparisons had been seen. The BraTS 2024 training data
include scans from UCSF. At UCSF-ALPTDG the time after radiotherapy had to be estimated from its start date. No cohort
included perfusion or diffusion imaging, which help separate progression from treatment effects in other studies
\citep{taylor2022}. Future work includes a reader study in which neuroradiologists judge the reports against the
scans, further cohorts such as Burdenko \citep{zolotova2023burdenko} and UCSD-PTGBM \citep{gagnon2026} to fill the
small groups of scans, and a wider test of the report checks.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

We built PRISM so that a reader can see, at each follow-up scan, what its score rests on. Keeping the scores separate,
and splitting each into the parts from its inputs, showed how much of the combined score came from the treatment dates
and how much from the images. A single AUC does not reveal this. A PRISM report places the imaging score beside the
clinical-history score, states how certain each is, and lets every number in it be traced to the scan's record.
