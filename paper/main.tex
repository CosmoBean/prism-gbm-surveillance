\begin{abstract}
PRISM predicts documented progression within 120 days of a glioma surveillance MRI and returns an audit record and a clinician report per scan. Its first version scored AUC 0.80 on one MU-Glioma-Post test split but averaged 0.72 over 500 similar splits; that split was easy because of its mix of scans before radiotherapy. Under repeated cross-validation, clinical and treatment-course variables reached 0.70 in grade 4, or 0.68 after removing leaks found by its own audit. Radiomics, frozen and fine-tuned deep encoders, resegmentation, a cleaner label and diffusion showed no measurable gain, and a locked model scored 0.61 externally. The phase of care drove discrimination: in surveillance after radiotherapy, the intended use, AUC was 0.60. Reports written from the audit record by a local language model passed a faithfulness check for all 249 grade 4 scans and contained every prespecified content item in 247; clinicians have not yet assessed them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

Most patients with high-grade glioma progress after surgery and chemoradiotherapy, and they are followed with MRI
every few months. At each surveillance scan the team asks how likely it is that progression will be documented
before the next decision point. Two things make this hard. Enhancement can increase after radiotherapy (RT)
without tumour growth. This pseudoprogression is most common in the first 12 weeks after chemoradiotherapy and
more frequent with MGMT promoter methylation \citep{brandes2008,taal2008}, which is why the RANO criteria ask for
confirmation of progression in that window \citep{wen2010,wen2023}. And in routine data, progression is a
documentation event. It depends on imaging, on symptoms and on when the next scan happens to be scheduled. A
model for this endpoint is a prognostic aid for deciding whether to bring the next scan forward or act on an
equivocal finding. For that use, a calibrated probability, an honest interval and a way to check what the model
knew matter as much as a high AUC.

Most imaging work addresses a neighbouring question: telling pseudoprogression from true progression at a lesion
that is already suspicious. There, machine learning models pool to an AUC of about 0.77 \citep{booth2022}, and
diffusion and perfusion measures reach 0.90 to 0.93 \citep{taylor2022}. Forward prediction from every
surveillance scan has had less attention. On the public MU-Glioma-Post dataset \citep{mahmoud2025}, a calibrated
radiomics model reported AUC 0.80 on one held-out set of patients \citep{christodoulou2026}.

Three problems can make such numbers mislead. Single train--test splits are unstable in small radiomics cohorts:
in one brain tumour dataset, one random split tested at 0.667 and another at 0.911 \citep{an2021}. Performance
drops on external data, by 0.10 or more for about a quarter of externally validated deep learning algorithms
\citep{yu2022}. And clinical comparators are rarely reported, although where they are, they hold their own
\citep{leone2023,gomaa2025,abler2025}. A fourth problem is specific to surveillance. A cohort mixes scans taken
before RT, during it, in the 12 weeks after it and in later follow-up, and event rates differ sharply between
these phases of care. Time since the end of RT was an input to both of the best documented clinical comparators
\citep{gomaa2025,leone2023}. A model that only recognises the phase can rank scans well whenever the test set
holds many easy phases, and treatment fields recorded once per patient can leak the future into a scan taken
before treatment started \citep{kapoor2023}.

We built PRISM (Post-treatment Response Intelligence and Surveillance Monitor) for this task. From what is known
on the scan day, it estimates the risk of documented progression within 120 days, records how the estimate was
made, and writes a short report that can be checked against that record. Its first evaluation, on one split of 30
test patients, gave an AUC of 0.80. We tested that number before relying on it, and each of the problems above
applied. This paper describes PRISM and what a rigorous evaluation showed: why the single split misled, that the
phase of care rather than MRI carries the signal, and how the audit record and the report make a modest model
transparent and checkable.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:2}

\subsection{PRISM}\label{sec:2.1}

PRISM takes one MRI (T1, T1c, T2 and FLAIR with a four-compartment tumour mask) and the patient's clinical tables
and returns three things for each scan: a risk, an audit record and a report (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). Every input
is read as it stood on the scan day.

\textbf{The risk.} A ridge logistic model combines the inputs available on the scan day: clinical and molecular
data, the treatment course and, optionally, imaging. The reference configuration is the \emph{core model} of
clinical and treatment-course variables (Section~\ref{sec:2.3}). Three imaging branches can be added: imaging
measurements (volumes, morphology and change), radiomics, and a SwinUNETR image encoder, frozen or fine-tuned.
Any branch can be switched off, which is how we ablate it.

\textbf{The audit record.} An audit layer wraps the model, locks it with a model card (code version, hashes of
every input table, each input's definition, source and as-of rule, intended and excluded uses) and writes one
record per scan. The record holds each input with its source and the day it became known; the phase of care; the
risk with a 95\% interval from 200 bootstrap refits; the exact contribution of each input group to the log odds; a
split conformal prediction set at $\alpha$ = 0.2; and referral flags with reasons. A scan is flagged if it falls in
the 12 weeks after RT, if an input lies outside the training range or a molecular marker is unknown, or if its
conformal set is \{0, 1\}.

\textbf{The report.} A local language model (Qwen3.8-27B at a pinned revision, greedy decoding, run offline on
our own GPUs) writes the report from one scan's audit record and nothing else, in the findings and impression form
of radiology reports. Every number must be copied from a record field and every sentence must cite the fields it
uses. Beyond the risk, the report states what a reader needs next to the number: how the risk compares with the
model's average, the risk at the previous scan, the phase of care and why it matters, the main drivers, every
active flag with its reason, what the model did not see (the images themselves), and any input not known on the
scan day. Diagnoses, RANO categories, management advice and unhedged certainty are forbidden. A deterministic
checker scores every sentence: cited fields must exist, every number must match a cited value, every active flag
must be mentioned, no inactive flag may be described as raised, and no forbidden pattern may appear.

PRISM's first version used a calibrated radiomics model, an untrained image encoder fused with it, and a report
from fixed RANO rules and a commercial language model. Its encoder carried no signal and its fusion did not beat
the radiomics input (Supplementary S7); the components above replace them.

\subsection{Data, outcome and intended use}\label{sec:2.2}

MU-Glioma-Post \citep{mahmoud2025} was the development cohort: MRI after surgery from a single centre, with
four-compartment masks (enhancing tumour, ET; non-enhancing core; surrounding FLAIR signal; resection cavity) and a
clinical workbook giving the day of each scan, surgery, RT, chemotherapy, first progression and death, and age,
sex, grade, IDH and MGMT. LUMIERE \citep{suter2022} was the external cohort: glioblastoma follow-up scans with an
expert RANO rating, automatic segmentations, IDH and MGMT, but no treatment dates. CFB-GBM \citep{cfbgbm} supported
a diffusion analysis: 264 patients imaged at RT planning and twice afterwards, with ADC and volumetric RANO 2.0
labels; we used only its released tables.

An index scan is an MRI before first documented progression and before later lines of treatment, with all four
sequences, a mask and a known date. Each patient contributes every eligible scan, and every analysis keeps a
patient's scans together. Grade 4 glioma was the primary population, fixed in advance (Table~\ref{tab:1}; study
design in Figure~\ref{fig:design}). The outcome is documented first progression within 120 days of the scan. A
scan counts as negative only if follow-up without progression reaches 120 days; otherwise it is censored, and
death without progression censors at the day of death. Discrimination is the AUC at 120 days weighted by the
inverse probability of censoring (IPCW AUC; \citealp{uno2007,blanche2013}). In LUMIERE, progression is the first
expert rating of progressive disease.

PRISM's intended use is surveillance after treatment has begun: scans taken on or after the documented start of
RT, a condition known on the scan day. Scans before RT are scored but flagged as outside the intended use
(Section~\ref{sec:3.3}).

Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI \citep{collins2024} and CLEAR (\citealp{kocak2023} \flag{[CHECK]}; Supplementary
S17). For each analysis called prespecified, the plan and its decision rule were written down before any of its
model outputs were seen, and for the later analyses committed to the public project repository first; the rules
and answers are collected in Table~\ref{tab:4} (Supplementary S1). None was registered with an external registry.

\subsection{Predictors}\label{sec:2.3}

All predictors are defined as of the scan day (Supplementary S4). Clinical: age, sex, IDH and MGMT. Timing: days
since diagnosis and since RT ended, a flag for the 12 weeks after RT and its interaction with MGMT methylation, and
visit number. Treatment: whether RT and chemotherapy had started and whether RT was complete, the prescription, and
the days of surgery and RT start. A treatment fact enters only once its documented start is on or before the scan
day. These variables form the core model. A compact alternative replaces timing and treatment with four phases of
care: before RT, during RT, within 12 weeks after RT, and later. Imaging measurements are compartment volumes,
morphology and change from earlier scans only. Radiomics cover all four sequences (Supplementary S5). Deep features
are pooled from SwinUNETR encoders with BraTS 2021 or BrainSegFounder weights (\citealp{cox2024} \flag{[CHECK]}),
with untrained weights as a control (Supplementary S6). Unknown values are imputed within each training fold.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:2.4}

\textbf{One split, then many.} The first evaluation used one split from PRISM's own split search: for a given seed,
20,000 random draws of 30 test patients, keeping the draw closest to 96 test scans and 53 positives. With the final
cohort it holds 126 training and 29 test patients (82 test scans). We rescored it with the final labels and reran
the same search for seeds 0 to 499, refitting the models on each split, to see where the first split ranks and how
each split's AUC relates to its mix of phases (Supplementary S8).

\textbf{Repeated cross-validation.} All main estimates use one shared set of 5 $\times$ 5 repeated folds, grouped
by patient and stratified by outcome and grade. Every fitted step (imputation, clipping, scaling, feature
selection, stacking and hyperparameters) is fitted inside the training fold \citep{varma2006,cawley2010}. The
estimate is the mean over repeats of the pooled held-out IPCW AUC; 95\% CIs and paired differences come from 1,000
bootstrap draws over patients, with all models scored on the same draws.

\textbf{What carries the signal.} Each branch is added to, or removed from, the core model. Radiomics enters
through 70 configurations and frozen deep features through 32; one configuration is chosen inside each outer
training fold by inner cross-validation (nested selection). To test the phase of care, we built bases without
imaging (R0 clinical; R1 plus timing; R2 clinical plus phase; R3 the core model), added imaging blocks to each, and
repeated the analysis within the surveillance cohort, the scans taken after RT was complete (grade 4: 167 scans, 84
patients). Because censoring turned out to be concentrated before RT, we re-estimated the censoring weights
separately within each phase and refitted after excluding patients whose RT was never documented.

\textbf{Giving imaging every chance.} When imaging added nothing, we tested five explanations. \emph{The label}:
progressions documented on clinical grounds alone were censored, giving an imaging-defined label. \emph{The
segmentation}: we checked volumes, labels and dates against their sources and resegmented all 594 complete MU scans
with the MONAI BraTS 2021 SwinUNETR and with BrainSegFounder. \emph{The cohort}: models trained on all MU scans were
applied unchanged to LUMIERE; without treatment dates, the transportable model used clinical variables, timing and
volumes (Supplementary S13). \emph{The modality}: in CFB-GBM, with one row per patient, we tested whether ADC
radiomics add to conventional radiomics for volumetric RANO 2.0 progression between the two scans after RT
(Supplementary S18). \emph{The encoder}: we fine-tuned the BraTS 2021 SwinUNETR on the 120-day label inside the
shared folds. The two deepest stages and a small head were trained on a cube around the lesion and cavity, with
fixed hyperparameters and early stopping on held-out training patients; four networks per outer fold gave
out-of-fold scores for stacking onto the core model (100 fine-tunes per arm). A copy trained on shuffled outcomes
showed what an uninformative score does to the stack (Supplementary S22).

\textbf{The audit layer} was evaluated on two prespecified quantities: conformal coverage on test scans with a
known outcome (P1), and whether withholding the least confident 30\% of scans raises the AUC of the rest more than
removing the same number at random within each phase (P2). It also probed shortcuts by refitting without the
indicators for unknown markers and with treatment or timing alone (Supplementary S15). Every fitted step, including
the conformal calibration split, stays inside the shared training folds.

\textbf{The reports} were evaluated on 249 grade 4 records from a cross-validation repeat not used in development.
The prompt and checker were developed on the 59 scans of other grades and then frozen. The primary quantities were
the share of reports the checker passes (no unsupported claim and every active flag stated) and the share that
contains every item of the required content listed above. We also ran PRISM's original prompt on the same records,
a smaller model (Qwen3.5-9B), and 30 perturbed records to check that a report follows a changed risk or flag. No
public set of surveillance reports exists, so we used 230 glioma MRI reports from RadGenome-Brain MRI and the MRI
descriptions of 181 published glioblastoma case reports (MultiCaRe) for form only (Supplementary S21).

\section{Results}\label{sec:3}

\subsection{Cohorts}\label{sec:3.1}

Table~\ref{tab:1} describes the cohorts. In the primary population, 249 grade 4 scans from 126 patients, 93 scans
were followed by documented progression within 120 days. The scans spread across the phases of care: 76 were taken
before RT, 6 during RT, 98 within 12 weeks after RT and 69 later.

\subsection{One split overstated performance}\label{sec:3.2}

Rescored with the final cohort and labels, the first split was generous to every model (Figure~\ref{fig:1}A;
Table~\ref{tab:2}). PRISM's initial radiomics model reached 0.80, but the core model, which uses no imaging,
reached 0.87, and age, sex, IDH and MGMT alone reached 0.79. The intervals were wide and overlapped.

The test set rewarded any model that recognises the phase of care. Of its 60 negative scans, 36 were taken before
RT and none of them was followed by documented progression, whereas 10 of the 68 scans before RT with a known
outcome in the whole cohort were (15\%; Figure~\ref{fig:1}B). So 60\% of the positive--negative pairs set a
progression scan against a fresh postoperative scan, and a rule with no training at all, ``the scan was taken after
RT'', scored 0.740 on this test set.

Across 500 splits of the same design (Figure~\ref{fig:2}A; Table~\ref{tab:2}), PRISM's initial radiomics pipeline,
reselecting its features on each split, scored higher on the first split than on 92\% of the others, and the core
model ranked at the 91st percentile. Each split's AUC followed the mix of phases in its test set (Figure~\ref{fig:2}B):
about two thirds of the first split's excess came from its mix of scans, and the rest from its particular
patients. Under repeated cross-validation the same core model scored 0.720 in all gliomas, against 0.871 on the
first split.

\subsection{The phase of care carries the signal}\label{sec:3.3}

Under repeated cross-validation, discrimination in grade 4 rose from 0.585 with clinical features alone to 0.644
with timing and 0.698 with treatment status: the core model (Figure~\ref{fig:3}A; Table~\ref{tab:2}). It was
calibrated in the large (ratio of observed to expected events 1.04; slope 0.89; Supplementary S12), and gradient
boosting did not beat ridge.

Most of this signal is the phase of care. Four phase categories recovered most of the gain over clinical features
(R2 0.664 against R0 0.585; Figure~\ref{fig:4}A), and treatment features alone scored 0.701, as high as the full
core model. Within the surveillance cohort, where the phase varies little, no base model discriminated well (0.549
to 0.604).

\textbf{The phase signal survives censoring, but lies between phases.} Of the 39 censored grade 4 scans, 38 were
taken before RT, from 31 patients whose RT and chemotherapy were never documented. The 37 patients in that group
were older, were scanned around surgery and had a median follow-up of 36 days after each scan (317 days for
patients with RT documented); 14 died without documented progression. Censoring weights estimated within each phase
raised the gain of R2 over R0 from +0.080 to +0.124, and excluding these patients left +0.057, so the prespecified
rule for a robust finding was met (Table~\ref{tab:4}). If every censored scan had progressed, the gain would vanish,
which seems unlikely but cannot be excluded. And the phase separates scans between phases, not within them:
counting only pairs within the same phase, R2 scored 0.577 against 0.595 for R0 (Supplementary S20).

\textbf{In the intended use.} On scans taken on or after the start of RT (173 grade 4 scans from 86 patients, 88
events, 1 censored), the corrected core model, trained on all scans, scored 0.601 (0.515--0.686): above chance,
but modest. It was calibrated in the large (observed to expected 1.01) but spread its predictions too widely
(slope 0.53), because part of their range encodes the difference between scans before and after RT; refitting within
the population did not improve discrimination (0.595). Within phases it scored 0.631 in the 12 weeks after RT and
0.583 later, and 0.616 over pairs within the same phase, so little of its discrimination here comes from ranking one
phase against another (Supplementary S23). The rule for scan-day eligibility removed 38 of the 39 censored scans and
all 14 deaths without progression, so the censoring question largely disappears in the intended use. In the full
cohort, phase-specific weights or counting deaths as non-events raised absolute AUCs (corrected model 0.734 and
0.706) but moved no imaging contrast by more than 0.011 (Table~\ref{tab:4}).

\subsection{Imaging adds nothing measurable}\label{sec:3.4}

No imaging branch showed a measurable gain (Table~\ref{tab:3}; Figure~\ref{fig:3}B). In the prespecified primary
analysis, nested radiomics changed the grade 4 core model by $-$0.032, and in grade 4 none of the 70 configurations
had a CI above 0. Frozen pretrained encoders changed it by $-$0.004. Their features did carry an image signal (0.652
alone, against 0.566 with untrained weights), but it tracked tumour volume (r = 0.58). Radiomics also made the
model overconfident (calibration slope 0.60). A model can give imaging credit without needing it: where volumes,
morphology and change were included they took about half of the SHAP credit, yet the models scored no better than
the core model (Figure~\ref{fig:3}C).

None of the five explanations rescued imaging (Table~\ref{tab:4}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{After RT}, where the phase cannot do the work, adding all imaging measurements to clinical and phase
variables moved the AUC from 0.549 to 0.610, within the range of the permutation null (p = 0.21;
Figure~\ref{fig:4}B).
\item \textbf{The label} was not the bottleneck: with progression on clinical grounds alone censored, the core model
plus imaging scored 0.722 against 0.696.
\item \textbf{Nor was the segmentation.} Our volumes reproduced the dataset's own table (r $\geq$ 0.996), yet across
all gliomas imaging separated the progression scan itself from verified negatives with an AUC of only 0.68, and ET
was larger at the progression scan than at the scan before in only 49\% of 74 pairs. Resegmentation agreed with the
original masks (median Dice 0.80 for ET) but did not improve detection (0.665; Figure~\ref{fig:imaging};
Supplementary S14).
\item \textbf{The external cohort} showed no imaging effect: on LUMIERE the locked model with volumes scored 0.608,
against 0.610 for clinical variables and timing alone (Supplementary S13).
\item \textbf{Diffusion} did not change the result: in 77 CFB-GBM patients with ADC (19 events), ADC radiomics added
+0.007 to conventional radiomics.
\item \textbf{Training the encoder on the task} did not help either. The fine-tuned image score alone reached 0.644,
again tracking tumour volume (r = 0.57) more than the core model (r = 0.13), and added +0.009 to the core model, at
most about +0.04 within its interval. The shuffled-outcome control alone scored 0.476, as intended, but its stack
gained +0.005, slightly above the limit of 0 we had set for a valid run: an uninformative score buys about half
of the observed gain. Other weights, all gliomas, and a network that also saw the previous scan gave $-$0.005 to
+0.017 (Supplementary S22).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What the audit found}\label{sec:3.5}

The audit layer reproduced the core model's predictions exactly, and the contributions summed to every logit.
Conformal coverage met its target (P1; Table~\ref{tab:4}), but almost every scan before RT received the set \{0\},
so all 5 grade 4 progressions before RT fell outside their sets. Selective prediction did not help (P2 not met):
withholding the least confident scans raised the AUC of the rest only by changing the phase mix, since it kept
97.6\% of scans taken before RT (Figure~\ref{fig:report}B).

The audit's value was what it found, none of which showed in the cross-validated AUC.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A missingness shortcut: IDH was unknown in none of 93 progression scans but in 6 of 117 scans without
progression, and removing the indicators for unknown markers lowered the grade 4 AUC by 0.023.
\item A treatment-course model: treatment features alone scored 0.701.
\item A molecular look-ahead: IDH and MGMT were used at 8 grade 4 scans taken 1 to 58 days before diagnosis.
\item The cause of external miscalibration: IDH was unknown in 40.9\% of LUMIERE scans, and the same shortcut
explained most of the gap between observed and expected events there.
\item A documentation leak: the flag for the 12 weeks after RT was missing when the RT end date was missing, which
told a scan whose RT would be documented later apart from one whose RT never would be.
\end{enumerate}
Removing these gave the \emph{corrected core model}, PRISM's reference configuration: 0.678 (0.596--0.755) in grade 4,
$-$0.020 against the original, equally well calibrated (observed to expected 1.04, slope 0.85), and 0.706 in all
gliomas. On LUMIERE it scored 0.635 against 0.608 and fixed the calibration (observed to expected 0.99, was 1.33;
slope 0.97, was 0.38).

\subsection{Reports that can be checked}\label{sec:3.6}

On the 249 evaluation records, the checker passed all 249 reports (1.000; Wilson 95\% CI 0.985--1.000), none
omitted any of the 262 active flags or contained forbidden content, and 247 contained every item of the required
content (Table~\ref{tab:5}; Figure~\ref{fig:report}A). The two exceptions named a flag without its reason. Reports
had a median of 190 words, about a minute to read, at a reading grade of 9.7. Grounding mattered more than the
model: PRISM's original prompt, given the same facts, gave management advice in 99\% of its reports, a diagnosis
in 51\% and a RANO category in 41\%, while the smaller model with the grounded prompt passed 243 of 249. Reports
followed the changed field in all 30 perturbed records. A first grounded prompt failed the checker in 13 of 249
reports for one reason, a risk sentence that stated the horizon without citing its field; the final prompt cites a
single field holding the whole risk statement (Supplementary S19 and S21). No clinician has yet read the reports.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:4}

PRISM was built to predict progression from surveillance MRI, and its evaluation changed what PRISM is: a modest
clinical and treatment-course model whose every estimate comes with a record of what it knew and a report that can
be checked against it. Three lessons emerged.

\textbf{A single split can measure the split.} The first 0.80 was real for that test set, but the test set was easy:
60\% of its pairs set a progression scan against a fresh postoperative scan, a contrast that any model able to
recognise the phase of care will win. The same split lifted the core model from 0.720 under repeated
cross-validation to 0.871. This adds a mechanism to the known instability of single splits \citep{an2021}: when
phases with very different event rates are mixed, the composition of a small test set explains much of the
variance, and a split search that matches the number of scans and positives does not control it. We suggest four
reporting rules for this task: repeated grouped cross-validation; the distribution over splits whenever a single
split is reported; results within phases of care; and a clinical comparator that knows the treatment course.

\textbf{The phase of care is a confounder.} A scan before RT is rarely followed by documented progression within
120 days; a scan in the 12 weeks after RT often is, partly because of pseudoprogression and the follow-up it
triggers \citep{brandes2008,taal2008,wen2023}. Four phase categories recovered most of the core model's gain, and
two published clinical comparators also found time since RT influential \citep{gomaa2025,leone2023}. Imaging models
that pool scans from all phases can therefore report high AUCs for recognising the phase rather than tumour biology.
The signal survived censoring corrections but ranks scans between phases, not within them, and part of it reflects
who stays in follow-up. This is why PRISM places every scan in its phase, flags the 12 weeks after RT, and is intended for scans from the start of RT onwards. There its AUC is 0.60, which shows how much of the
apparent discrimination on mixed cohorts comes from the phase of care.

\textbf{Imaging, as tested here, showed no gain.} Even at the scan where progression was documented, volumes
separated it from negatives with an AUC of only 0.68. Three features of the data explain this. The label is a
documentation event: one in eight grade 4 events was documented on clinical grounds alone. Volume series were noisy:
ET changed more between scans less than 14 days apart (median 3.2-fold) than between scans 45 to 90 days apart (Figure~\ref{fig:imaging}C--D). And
the cohort is small: by the Riley criteria even the core model would need about ten times more scans
\citep{riley2020}. Small effects cannot be excluded: the upper confidence bounds for imaging reach 0.02 to 0.16, and
training the encoder on the label gave +0.009. Where imaging performs well in the literature, it uses diffusion and
perfusion to classify a lesion that is already suspicious \citep{kim2019,taylor2022}, a different question from
forward risk at every scan. PRISM keeps its imaging branches as optional modules so that larger cohorts can test them
again. Attribution is no guide: imaging took half of the SHAP credit without improving discrimination, so a claim of
imaging value needs a paired difference with an interval.

\textbf{What PRISM offers.} At an AUC of about 0.70, PRISM does not offer better triage: abstention raised the AUC of
the remaining scans only by changing their phase mix, and the conformal sets met their target mainly by calling every
scan before RT negative. What it offers is a record of what the model knew on the scan day, where the scan sits in
the treatment course, how uncertain the estimate is and which inputs drove it. Running the audit found a missingness
shortcut, a molecular look-ahead, a documentation leak and the cause of the external miscalibration, none of which
the AUC revealed. Grounding the language model in that record and checking every sentence against it gave reports
that restate the record without verdicts or advice, and that put the number in context: how it compares with the
average, how it changed since the last scan, what the model could not see and why the phase matters. We think this is
the honest use of a modest model: to make its reasoning and its failure modes visible, not to claim better decisions.
Whether clinicians find the reports useful is the next question; a protocol for a randomised reader study is in
Supplementary S21.

\textbf{Comparison with other work.} The grade 4 core model (0.698) is close to clinical comparators for
neighbouring tasks (0.69 in \citealp{leone2023}; 0.727 externally in \citealp{gomaa2025}). It is below the single
test set result of 0.80 on MU-Glioma-Post \citep{christodoulou2026}, which used a different label (the progression
status of each scan) and which we did not re-evaluate. Higher AUCs for forward recurrence prediction elsewhere use
different cohorts, horizons and units, and some fall externally (0.887 to 0.762 in \citealp{chen2026}).

\subsection*{Limitations}

The development cohort comes from one centre, with 126 grade 4 patients and 93 events at 120 days, far below
recommended sample sizes. The outcome is documented progression from routine records, not a blinded RANO read, and
its timing depends on scheduling. MU images carry no acquisition metadata, so scanner effects could not be studied.
Censoring was concentrated in patients whose RT was never documented; phase-specific weights and their exclusion
left the phase finding intact, but censoring that depends on the outcome itself cannot be ruled out. Our bootstrap
intervals resample fixed held-out predictions and do not include the variability of refitting, so they are likely
too narrow. The external cohort is small, uses a different segmenter and lacks treatment dates, so the full core
model was not tested externally. Several imaging tests were added after the first imaging result was known,
although each had its rule fixed before its own run. Radiomics used a fixed bin count rather than the fixed bin
width recommended by IBSI \citep{zwanenburg2020}. The fine-tuned encoder was trained on about 100 scans per network
with fixed hyperparameters, and its shuffled-outcome control slightly missed its validity limit. The ablation and
phase analyses use the original core model; the corrected model scores 0.020 lower. The report checker is
lexical: it verifies numbers, citations, flags and forbidden patterns, not meaning. The required content was defined
by us, so meeting it shows that the reports say what we specified, not that they help; the public reference reports
describe preoperative or published cases rather than routine surveillance; and no clinician has assessed the reports. Finally, at an AUC of 0.60 in its intended use, PRISM is not ready to
guide care; a triage model would need larger multicentre cohorts, a label read from images by experts, and perfusion
or diffusion imaging, and would need to be tested prospectively.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

PRISM's first AUC of 0.80 came from one favourable split. Under repeated cross-validation its core of clinical and
treatment-course variables reached about 0.70, or 0.68 after removing leaks found by its own audit, and radiomics,
deep features (frozen or fine-tuned), resegmentation, a cleaner label and diffusion added no measurable
discrimination. The phase of care drives discrimination on this task and should be reported as a stratum and
treated as a confounder. In surveillance from the start of RT, PRISM's intended use, its corrected core model reached an AUC of 0.60, which
is not enough to guide care. What PRISM provides is a way to build and test such models honestly: every prediction
is recorded, explained and reported in a form that can be checked, and the audit exposed leaks that the AUC hid.
