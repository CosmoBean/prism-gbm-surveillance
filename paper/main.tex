\begin{abstract}
A score that ranks follow up MRI scans after glioma treatment can do well because of when scans were taken.
PRISM, a local system, gives each scan three scores, statements of how far to trust them and a
report checked against its record. It runs three audits adapted from known techniques. An
exact split of the AUC separates comparisons within and across time windows after radiotherapy, conformal coverage is
measured within groups of comparable scans, and a verifier binds every number and direction word of a report to the
record. On the public MU-Glioma-Post cohort, the clinical-history model's AUC of 0.82 combined 0.93 across windows
with 0.64 within one. Calibrating sets within groups helped no more than enlarging them. The verifier rejected every
value placed under the wrong label, including independently written errors, but only a third of
direction errors in new wording. No clinician has read the reports.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

% Sources: refs wen2023, taal2008; STYLE.md section 10 (2026-11-03 rules).
After surgery and chemoradiotherapy for glioblastoma and other aggressive gliomas, each follow up MRI is read against
earlier scans and the course of treatment. Enhancement can grow because the tumour grows or because of treatment,
and the second is most common in the first 12 weeks after radiotherapy \citep{wen2023,taal2008}. Models proposed for
this setting give each scan a score that ranks it by how closely it resembles scans at which progression was later
documented. A score is useful only if the reader can tell what it rests on and how far to trust it at the scan in
front of them.

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md M1; refs janes2009, kallus2019, geirhos2020, zech2018, badgeley2019, matos2026.
Three properties of such a score are rarely measured. The first is where its discrimination comes from. Follow up
schedules tie the time of a scan to its label, since scans soon after radiotherapy are rarely the ones at which
progression is documented. An AUC pools every pair of a scan with and without progression, so a model can rank well by
recognising the time window of a scan, a form of shortcut learning \citep{geirhos2020,zech2018,badgeley2019}.
Covariate adjusted ROC analysis has long shown that a pooled AUC is inflated when a covariate is linked to both the
marker and the outcome \citep{janes2009}, and the AUC splits exactly into pairs within and across groups
\citep{kallus2019,matos2026}. The second property is how far to trust a score at one scan, which an average coverage
over a cohort does not give for the scans that matter, and the third is whether a report generated from the score
states only what the record holds.

% Sources: OUTLINE.md (Methods rewrite 2026-11-15); NUMBERS.md 3--7.
PRISM is a system that makes progression scores inspectable. For each follow up scan it builds one record that holds
tumour volumes measured from segmentation masks, three separately fitted scores, statements of confidence and the
source of every value, and a local language model writes a report from that record alone. We developed PRISM on the
public MU-Glioma-Post cohort, fitted its models once, and applied them unchanged in the LUMIERE and UCSF-ALPTDG cohorts.
This paper adapts three known techniques into audits that PRISM runs at each centre where it is tested, and measures
what each shows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item A context audit of discrimination (Section~\ref{sec:3.1}). The AUC splits exactly into pairs of scans from the
same time window after radiotherapy and pairs from different windows \citep{kallus2019}. We report both parts for every
model at each centre, with the change in each part when a block of inputs is added and the part that the time window
alone could supply.
\item A coverage audit by group (Section~\ref{sec:3.2}). We measure the coverage of conformal sets within groups of
comparable scans, defined by the time after radiotherapy and the state of the enhancing tumour, and test group
calibration with a fixed fallback to coarser groups \citep{vovk2022book,ding2023} against marginal sets enlarged to
the same size.
\item A verifier that binds each claim of a generated report to the record entity it refers to
(Section~\ref{sec:3.3}), extending deterministic checks of generated clinical text \citep{zeba2026,pham2026}. Every
number and direction word must agree with a cited field about the same tumour region or score. It is measured by the
share of planted errors it rejects in each class, including errors written by someone who had not seen its rules,
and by how many reports that passed the earlier checker it rejects, for a language model and a fixed template.
\end{enumerate}

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md M1--M3.
On MU the clinical-history and combined models drew most of their discrimination from pairs in different windows,
which the window alone ranked as well, while the imaging model ranked scans about as well within a window as across
windows. The marginal conformal sets covered the hardest group of scans least, and calibrating within groups did not
cover it better than enlarging the sets. The verifier caught every wrong label but only the direction words on its
list.

\section{PRISM}\label{sec:2}

% Sources: SYSTEM_PLAN.md section 1.
PRISM turns one follow up scan and the patient's clinical record into a structured record and a report in five stages
(Figure~\ref{fig:1}). It measures the tumour, scores the scan with three models, attaches the confidence statements,
writes every value into one record with its source, and has a local language model write the report, which the
verifier then accepts or rejects. Table~\ref{tab:models} lists every score in the paper.

\subsection{Inputs and measurements}\label{sec:2.1}

% Sources: DETECTION_TASK.md (Plan, B1); UCSF_IMAGING.md; NUMBERS.md 2.1; refs mahmoud2025, suter2022, fields2024.
PRISM takes three inputs for a scan. The first is the segmentation of the current and earlier scans, which gives the
volumes of enhancing tumour, FLAIR hyperintensity, necrosis and the resection cavity. We used the images and masks as
released with each cohort \citep{mahmoud2025,suter2022,fields2024}, so every volume can be traced to a public file.
From the masks and images PRISM computes 27 measurements. Nineteen describe the current scan (the log volumes of six
compartments and 13 measures of shape and of T1 contrast and FLAIR intensity relative to normal appearing brain,
Supplementary Table~S47), and eight describe change since the previous scan and since the patient's smallest volume.
The second input is the treatment context as of the scan day (days since diagnosis and surgery, and the start, end and
dose of radiotherapy and chemotherapy). From it PRISM places the scan in a time window after radiotherapy (before its
end, within 12 weeks after its end, later, or unknown when the dates are missing). The third input is the patient's
age, sex, and IDH mutation and MGMT promoter methylation status. Every input is taken as of the scan day, so no
information from a later visit enters a score.

\subsection{Three models}\label{sec:2.2}

% Sources: prism/models/formulations.py; prism/experiments/c3_radiomics.py (BASE_PRE); NUMBERS.md 2.1.
PRISM scores each scan with three models and shows all three. The clinical-history model uses the treatment context
and the patient characteristics, the imaging model uses the 27 measurements and no dates, and the combined model uses
both. Keeping the first two apart is what lets the record show which kind of evidence a score follows. Each is a
logistic regression with an L2 penalty, chosen because its coefficients can be read, and deep networks tuned on MU
did not improve on it (Table~\ref{tab:2}). The inputs are standardised, and the penalty is chosen from six values of the
inverse strength $C$ (0.01 to 3) by threefold cross validation grouped by patient, minimising the log loss. Where a
cohort lacks treatment dates, PRISM uses a reduced clinical-history model with the inputs that cohort has. LUMIERE's
masks have three compartments, so for LUMIERE we fitted an imaging model on MU with only those three volumes and their
change. Because the imaging model needs only scans and masks, PRISM gives an imaging score in a cohort with no
treatment dates.

\subsection{Record and report}\label{sec:2.3}

% Sources: prism/report/facts_v3_1.py; prism/report/prompts_v3_1.py; REPORT_V3_1.md; Supplementary S19, S24.
Every value PRISM computes for a scan enters one structured record under a fixed key, with its source. A local language
model (Qwen3.8-27B, greedy decoding) writes the report from that record alone, so no patient data leave the hospital's
hardware. Decoding is constrained to a JSON schema in which every sentence carries its section and the keys of the
record fields it uses, and the model sees no images and does no arithmetic. The record also carries two descriptive
statements. One says whether the clinical-history and imaging scores fall on the same side of their cohort medians. The
other is a reliability card \citep{mitchell2019} that gives, for the scan's situation (time window and state of the
enhancing tumour), how many scans with and without progression the models were tested on and, with at least 10 of
each (the tested tier), their AUCs, and otherwise only the counts (few cases). The card is built without any scan of
the patient it describes. The report follows the content of
the BT-RADS follow up template \citep{weinberg2018} without its category or advice, which PRISM does not give
(Supplementary Section~S24).

\section{Methods}\label{sec:3}

\subsection{Splitting discrimination by context}\label{sec:3.1}

% Sources: METHODS_PLAN.md M1; prism/analyses/methods.py; refs kallus2019, janes2009, borkan2019.
Let $P$ and $N$ be the scans with and without progression, $s$ a score, and $c$ a context label for each scan, here
the time window after radiotherapy. The AUC is the share of pairs ranked correctly,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{1}{|P|\,|N|} \sum_{i \in P} \sum_{j \in N} K(s_i, s_j), \qquad
K(a, b) = \mathbb{1}[a > b] + \tfrac12\, \mathbb{1}[a = b].
\end{equation}
The pairs split into $W = \{(i, j) : c_i = c_j\}$, two scans from the same window, and its complement $B$. With
$A_W$ and $A_B$ the same average over $W$ and over $B$, and $w = |W| / (|P|\,|N|)$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC} = w\, A_W + (1 - w)\, A_B
\end{equation}
holds exactly \citep{kallus2019}. $A_W$ is the discrimination with the window held fixed, the pooled within stratum AUC
of covariate adjusted ROC analysis \citep{janes2009}. $A_B$ mixes what the window and the score each contribute. To
show how much of $A_B$ the window alone could supply, we score every scan by the progression rate of its window in the
training data (the context only score), which ties all pairs in $W$ and ranks pairs in $B$ by window alone. A model
whose 95\% CI for $A_B - A_W$ lies above zero draws part of its discrimination from the context. To see where a block of inputs adds discrimination, we report the change in $A_W$ and $A_B$ when it is added,
here clinical history added to images and images added to clinical history.

At MU the window comes from the treatment dates (during radiotherapy, within 12 weeks after its end, and later, since scans
before radiotherapy are outside the intended use). LUMIERE records only the rater's flag for scans within three months
of radiotherapy, which gives two windows, and for the context only score a flagged scan takes MU's rate for its first
12 weeks and any other scan MU's rate over the other windows. Intervals come from 2,000 bootstrap samples of patients,
and at MU each sample averages over the five repeats of cross validation. The identity was checked on every sample.

\subsection{Conformal coverage within groups}\label{sec:3.2}

% Sources: prism/audit/conformal.py; METHODS_PLAN.md M2; refs angelopoulos2023, vovk2022book, romano2020, ding2023,
% barber2021limits, mehrtens2025.
A conformal set names the outcomes a score cannot rule out \citep{angelopoulos2023}. Write $\hat p(1) = \hat p$ for a
scan's score and $\hat p(0) = 1 - \hat p$. For each calibration scan $i$ with outcome $y_i$ the nonconformity score is
$s_i = 1 - \hat p_i(y_i)$, the probability the model did not give to what happened. Because a patient contributes
several scans, each calibration scan is weighted by one over the number of that patient's calibration scans. With
$\alpha = 0.2$ and $n$ calibration patients, the threshold $\hat q$ is the smallest $s_i$ whose cumulative weight
reaches $(1 - \alpha)(n + 1)$, the added one standing for the new patient. A new scan receives
\begin{equation}
C(\hat p) = \{\, y \in \{0, 1\} : 1 - \hat p(y) \le \hat q \,\}.
\end{equation}
As planned, the combined model was refitted on 70\% of the training patients and calibrated on the other 30\%, inside
each training fold on MU and once on all MU training patients for the external cohorts. If a new patient is
exchangeable with the calibration patients, about 80\% of their sets contain the true outcome on average, but not
necessarily within a subgroup, and because scans are weighted by patient the guarantee is approximate \citep{romano2020,barber2021limits}.

Group-conditional (Mondrian) sets \citep{vovk2022book} compute $\hat q$ separately within each group $g$, so the
guarantee holds within the group under exchangeability there. Our groups are the situations of the reliability card,
the time window crossed with the state of the enhancing tumour (absent below 0.1\,cc, without comparison when there is
no previous scan, increased when it grew by at least 1\,cc and 40\%, or not increased). Most groups are small, so each
scan is calibrated at the finest of three levels that holds at least 10 calibration patients, its group, then its
enhancement state over all windows, then all calibration scans, which gives the planned marginal set. A scan whose
window is unknown in an external cohort starts at the second level. The level used is written into the record, so a
reader sees whether the set was calibrated on comparable scans. Merging small groups into coarser ones follows
clustered conformal prediction \citep{ding2023}. No guarantee carries over to an external cohort unless its scans are
exchangeable with MU's, which a different centre need not satisfy \citep{mehrtens2025}. Group calibration can raise
coverage in a group simply by making sets larger, so after the planned run we also compared it with marginal sets at
the $\alpha$ that gives the same share of sets holding both outcomes, and with sets calibrated separately for each
outcome (exploratory).

\subsection{Binding report claims to the record}\label{sec:3.3}

% Sources: prism/report/checker.py; prism/report/verifier.py (v1.1, blob d4b93db5); METHODS_PLAN.md M3; METHODS_RESULTS.md
% M3; refs zeba2026, wiseman2017, kryscinski2020, pham2026.
The report is a list of claims, each a sentence with the record fields it cites. The earlier checker of PRISM, frozen
before any evaluation report was generated, rejects a report that cannot be parsed, cites a field that does not exist,
states a number found in no cited field, omits a raised flag, misstates the conformal set or the agreement, omits the
card's tier where evidence is thin, leaves out a score, quotes a performance figure absent from the card, or offers a
diagnosis, a response category, advice or certainty words. A rejected report is not repaired. Deterministic checks of
this kind compare generated text with a source record \citep{zeba2026,wiseman2017}, and the checker accepts a number
if it equals a number in any cited field. It therefore accepts a sentence that gives the FLAIR volume as the enhancing
volume while citing both fields, or that writes ``larger'' where the record says ``smaller''.

The verifier adds two rules that bind a claim to what it refers to. A sentence is split into clauses at commas,
semicolons, colons and the word ``and''. An entity is a tumour region (enhancing tumour, FLAIR hyperintensity or
oedema, necrosis, cavity) or one of the three scores, and each number and direction word in a clause is assigned to the
nearest entity named in that clause. From the cited fields the verifier collects the values and directions that belong
to each entity, those of a field whose key names the entity and those in a clause of a cited text field that names it.
The first rule requires every number assigned to an entity to equal, up to its written rounding, a value of that
entity. The second requires every direction word (larger, increased, grew, rose, higher, and their opposites) to agree
with every cited direction of its entity, read from a direction word or from the sign of a change field, and a
direction word about a tumour region with no cited direction is unsupported. The verifier passes a report only if the
frozen checker and both rules pass.

We fixed the rules, and the planted errors that would test them, in a plan written before any report was checked.
The rules as first written failed. They looked for the entity only in the name of a cited field, but most fields are
sentences such as ``enhancing tumour 3.5\,cc larger than at the previous scan'' whose entity is named in the text, so
every real report was rejected. We then let the rules read the entity from the text of a cited field as well, tested
this change only on the synthetic records and the 48 reports used to develop the prompt, froze it, and applied it once
to the evaluation reports. To measure the verifier we
introduced one error at a time into correct reports, with 24 kinds of error in seven classes (numbers, citations,
flags, conformal and agreement statements, the card's tier, forbidden wording, and binding), following error
injection used to evaluate factuality checks \citep{kryscinski2020,gabriel2021,rao2025rexerr}. Three binding errors
were held out, defined in the plan but not used when the rules were written. Errors were planted in correct reports for
52 synthetic records and, wherever the pattern occurs, in the 276 evaluation reports written by the language model and
in the same records written by a fixed template that copies each field into a set sentence. A rule closes a blind spot
if it rejects at least 95\% of the synthetic errors of that kind. Because we wrote both the rules and these errors,
we afterwards had a separate agent, which had not seen the rules, write further errors into the synthetic reports in
its own words (wrong directions, values under the wrong entity, scores on the wrong side of their median) together
with correct paraphrases as controls (exploratory).

\section{Results}\label{sec:5}

\subsection{The locked models ranked scans at two external centres}\label{sec:5.1}

% Sources: DETECTION_TASK.md; UCSF_RESULTS.md Part A--C; NUMBERS.md 3, 4, 12C, 15.
The three models ranked scans on MU, and the combined and imaging models kept ranking them, locked, at both external
centres (Figure~\ref{fig:2}). On MU the clinical-history model detected the progression scan with an AUC of 0.82 (95\%
CI 0.75--0.90), the imaging model with 0.71 (0.63--0.78) and clinical data alone with 0.53 (0.45--0.61). The combined
score also reached 0.82, with no gain over the clinical-history model. Locked, the reduced combined model reached 0.77
(0.68--0.86) at LUMIERE. At UCSF images from one scan reached 0.80 (0.69--0.89) and the locked combined model 0.86
(0.76--0.93), while the reduced clinical-history model, whose radiotherapy flag is approximated there, scored 0.56
(0.42--0.69). Neither a second segmentation of MU nor deep networks tuned on MU improved detection
(Table~\ref{tab:2}). The released volumes are noisy, since between consecutive MU scans within 30 days of each other
the enhancing volume changed by a median factor of 2.6, so the report calls them indicative.

What a score followed differed between the centres. At UCSF the label coincided with enhancement, since UCSF rated
enhancement as increased at 27 of the 29 progression scans and dated progression a median of 7 days after the scan.
Enhancing volume alone, with no fitted model, reached 0.88 (0.79--0.95) there, above the point estimates of the locked
imaging models and above its own AUC at MU and LUMIERE (Figure~\ref{fig:2}). At MU and LUMIERE the time window is
recorded, and the next section asks how much of the models' discrimination it carries.

\subsection{Most of the clinical-history discrimination came from comparing time windows}\label{sec:5.2}

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md M1 and E1.
On MU the clinical-history model ranked pairs from different time windows far better than pairs from the same
window, and the imaging model did not (Figure~\ref{fig:3}). At MU 38\% of pairs fell within one window. The
clinical-history model ranked 0.93 (0.88--0.97) of the pairs across windows correctly and 0.64 (0.51--0.78) of the
pairs within one, a difference of 0.29 (0.16--0.43). Ranking scans by their window's progression rate alone, with no
other input, also ranked 0.93 of the pairs across windows correctly, so the window could supply all of the
clinical-history model's advantage there. The imaging model ranked 0.68 (0.58--0.77) within a window and 0.73 across,
a difference whose interval includes zero. Nine in ten pairs within a window come from the later window (56 scans
with progression and 32 without), so the within window AUC is close to the AUC of scans taken later than 12 weeks
after radiotherapy (exploratory, Supplementary Section~S26). LUMIERE gives a weaker test, because only 1 of the 28
scans its rater flagged as within three months of radiotherapy had progression, and the reduced clinical-history
model takes the flag as an input. Its across window AUC of 0.96 against 0.59 within one therefore shows mainly how
much the flag carries. The imaging model at LUMIERE ranked scans better within a window (0.71) than across (0.51), and
enhancing volume behaved like the imaging model at both centres.

The split also shows where each block of inputs adds discrimination. Adding clinical history to images raised the
AUC by 0.11 (0.03--0.19) at MU and 0.12 (0.04--0.21) at LUMIERE, and at both centres the whole gain lay in pairs
across windows (within one window $-$0.02 and $+$0.01, with intervals that include zero). Adding images to clinical
history gained nothing at MU. At LUMIERE it raised the within window AUC by 0.12 (0.00--0.23), an interval that reaches
zero, and the overall AUC by 0.08 ($-$0.01 to $+$0.16). A pooled AUC reports these gains as one number, so the same
score can look equally good at two centres while resting on different comparisons.

\subsection{The marginal sets covered the hardest group least, and group calibration did not fix it}\label{sec:5.3}

% Sources: NUMBERS.md 6 and 12B; SYSTEM_RESULTS.md S1.
The planned marginal sets of the combined score met the coverage rule on point estimates at all three centres,
covering 82\%, 87\% and 78\% of scans at MU, LUMIERE and UCSF, but not within subgroups. At UCSF they covered 97\% of
progression scans and 65\% (51\% to 79\%) of scans without progression. At MU the group of scans later than 12 weeks
after radiotherapy with increased enhancement, where progression and its mimics are hardest to tell apart, was covered
at 68\% (55\% to 81\%).

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md M2 and E2; Supplementary Section S26.
Calibrating within groups changed which groups were covered more than how well the worst ones were
(Figure~\ref{fig:4}). At MU it raised coverage in the later group without increased enhancement from 78\% to 87\%
and lowered it in scans within 12 weeks of radiotherapy without a previous scan from 99\% to 84\%. In the later group
with increased enhancement coverage rose to 72\% (59\% to 85\%), and in the smallest later group, without
enhancement (9 scans), it stayed at 67\%. The group-conditional sets held both outcomes more often (31\% of MU scans
against 25\%, 42\% at LUMIERE against 38\%), and 4\% of MU sets were empty. A marginal set enlarged to the same share
of two outcome sets (exploratory, $\alpha = 0.175$) covered the two worst groups at 69\% and 74\%, at least as well,
and a set calibrated separately for each outcome covered the increased enhancement group at 64\%. The gain in the worst
group therefore came from larger sets, not from calibration within groups. At MU 29\% of scans were calibrated in
their own group, 45\% in their enhancement state and 26\% over all scans. For the imaging model at LUMIERE the largest
shortfall grew, from 16 to 18 percentage points (Supplementary Section~S26).

\subsection{The verifier caught every wrong label but only the direction words on its list}\label{sec:5.4}

% Sources: REPORT_V3_1.md (S5--S7); REPORT_V3.md; NUMBERS.md 7.
All 406 reports written by Qwen3.8-27B for patients not used to develop the prompt passed the planned checker (114 at
MU, 162 at LUMIERE and 130 at UCSF), after one revision of the prompt made because every LUMIERE report first failed.
All 220 reports in situations with few cases or none said so. When one field was changed in 30 records, 28 reports
followed the change, against a planned minimum of 27. Of six other local models given the same prompt, only Gemma-4 31B also had every report pass (Figure~\ref{fig:5}B). The reports were generated again with this paper's model names,
and these 276 MU and LUMIERE reports, which the verifier was tested on, also all passed (Supplementary Section~S25).

The binding rules rejected every planted error of the planned set that they target, including the held out ones

opposite direction and every swap of the enhancing and FLAIR volumes. It rejected the two held out errors that give a

requires. Because the same authors wrote these errors and the rules, a separate agent that had not seen the rules
wrote 572 more variants of the synthetic reports. The verifier rejected all 156 values placed under the wrong entity,
against 12\% for the checker, and none of 104 correct paraphrases. It rejected only 32\% of 208 direction errors phrased
in words outside its list (such as regressed, receded or a reduction), and neither the verifier nor the checker caught
more than 14\% of 104 scores placed on the wrong side of their median in new wording. We did not widen the word list,
since tuning it on these errors would spend them as a test. One planned blind spot also remains. A tier word changed to
``tested'' where the card has few cases matches no rule, and 33\% to 45\% of such real reports passed.

Of the 276 MU and LUMIERE reports of each writer that had passed the checker, the verifier rejected one written by the
language model at MU (1 of 114 there) and none written by the template. That report failed the direction rule and was
not inspected, because the reports are not read outside the local hardware, so we cannot say whether it was wrong.
The language model's reports were a quarter shorter (median 308 against 413 words) and scored higher on a readability
formula (Flesch reading ease 48.5 against 39.4), and whether either reads better to a clinician is not known.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:6}

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md M1, E1; NUMBERS.md 3--5.
The locked models of PRISM ranked scans at two other centres, but the split of their AUC shows that on MU the
clinical-history and combined models did so mostly by comparing scans from different time windows after
radiotherapy, which the window alone ranked as well. The imaging model and enhancing volume ranked scans about as
well within a window as across windows. A pooled AUC cannot make this distinction, and models with equal AUC can
behave differently on new data \citep{damour2022}. The split is a published identity \citep{kallus2019}, covariate
adjusted ROC analysis made the same point for pooled ROC curves \citep{janes2009}, and a study of mammography found
pooled AUC misleading in the same way \citep{tsue2023}. What this paper adds is its use as a routine audit of a
surveillance score at each centre where it is tested, with the part the context alone supplies and the change in each
part when a block of inputs is added. Anyone who reports a glioma progression model could report $A_W$ and $A_B$ next
to the AUC at no cost, using whatever context their cohort records. The audit is only as good as the recorded context,
and at LUMIERE a single rater's flag with one progression scan inside it gave a weak test.

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md M2, E2; NUMBERS.md 6.
A coverage target met on average can hide groups in which the sets are wrong more often, and in this study the
marginal sets covered the later scans with increased enhancement, where progression is hardest to tell from its
mimics, least. Calibrating within groups did not cover that group better than enlarging the sets, so in cohorts this
small its main effect was to move coverage from groups that were over covered to others, and the gain we first saw
came from larger sets. What group calibration still offers is that the record states which scans a set was calibrated
on. Larger cohorts, or pooled calibration across centres, would be needed for a guarantee within the groups that
matter, and a centre adopting PRISM would calibrate on its own scans.

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md M3, E3; refs pham2026, mansour2026, nam2026.
A verifier for generated reports is only as good as the errors it has been tested on. Binding each number to the
entity it describes caught every wrong label, including labels written by someone who had not seen the rules, without
rejecting correct paraphrases. Binding direction words caught only the words on its list, which is the weakness of any
fixed vocabulary, and a wider list would need a fresh set of independent errors to test it. Other deterministic gates
for clinical text forbid numbers altogether \citep{pham2026} or trace numbers to a measurement file without measuring
the check \citep{mansour2026}, and planted errors have been used to evaluate gates of this kind \citep{nam2026}. The
template passed every check too, so the guarantees come from the verifier, and the language model adds only shorter
text. The verifier cannot see errors outside the classes tested, such as a misleading emphasis, and it cannot tell
whether the segmentation is right.

% Sources: OUTLINE.md (Discussion 5); NUMBERS.md 10; UCSF_AUDIT.md.
PRISM was developed on one public cohort, and the two external tests are its only evidence that the ranking
transfers. The label is progression as each centre documented it, and no scan was read again. The three audits ran on
MU and LUMIERE only, because UCSF-ALPTDG was no longer on our hardware. In the first 12 weeks after radiotherapy, when
progression and treatment effects are hardest to tell apart, MU holds too few progression scans to test any score,
and the report says so. No clinician has read the reports, so whether the record changes a reading is untested. The
next steps are to run the three audits on UCSF-ALPTDG, to pool the rare situations across cohorts, including the
Burdenko and UCSD-PTGBM datasets, and to run the reader study we have designed.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

% Sources: OUTLINE.md (Methods rewrite 2026-11-15).
PRISM gives each follow up scan after glioma treatment a record that shows where its scores' discrimination comes
from, how its conformal set was calibrated and a report whose numbers are bound to the record. On the public
development cohort most of the clinical-history model's discrimination came from comparing scans taken at different
times after radiotherapy, which a pooled AUC does not show.
