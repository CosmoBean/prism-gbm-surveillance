\begin{abstract}
PRISM predicts documented progression within 120 days of a glioma surveillance MRI and returns an audit record and a grounded clinician report for every scan. Its first version scored AUC 0.80 on one test split of 30 patients from MU-Glioma-Post, and we tested that number before building on it. Over 500 splits of the same design the pipeline averaged 0.72; our split was easy because of its mix of scans taken before radiotherapy. Under repeated cross-validation, a core of clinical and treatment-course variables reached 0.70 in grade 4 glioma. Radiomics, deep features, resegmentation, a cleaner label and diffusion showed no measurable gain, and a locked model scored 0.61 on an external cohort. The phase of care drove discrimination. The audit layer made each prediction traceable and exposed a missingness shortcut, but did not improve triage. Of 249 reports written by a local language model, 236 passed a faithfulness check.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

Most patients with high-grade glioma progress after surgery and chemoradiotherapy, and they are followed with MRI
every few months. At each surveillance scan the team asks how likely it is that progression will be documented
before the next decision point. Two things make this hard. Enhancement can increase after radiotherapy (RT)
without tumour growth. This pseudoprogression is most common in the first 12 weeks after chemoradiotherapy and
more frequent with MGMT promoter methylation \citep{brandes2008,taal2008}, which is why the RANO criteria ask for
confirmation of progression in that window \citep{wen2010,wen2023}. And in routine data, progression is a
documentation event. It depends on imaging, on symptoms and on when the next scan happens to be scheduled. A
model for this endpoint is a prognostic aid for deciding whether to bring the next scan forward or act on an
equivocal finding. For that use, a calibrated probability, an honest interval and a way to check what the model
knew matter as much as a high AUC.

Most imaging work addresses a neighbouring question: telling pseudoprogression from true progression at a lesion
that is already suspicious. There, machine learning models pool to an AUC of about 0.77 \citep{booth2022}, and
diffusion and perfusion measures reach 0.90 to 0.93 \citep{taylor2022}. Forward prediction from every
surveillance scan has had less attention. On the public MU-Glioma-Post dataset \citep{mahmoud2025}, a calibrated
radiomics model reported AUC 0.80 on one held-out set of patients \citep{christodoulou2026}, and a model of volume
increase at the next scan reported 0.728 under nested cross-validation \citep{rai2026}
\flag{[CHECK: Rai 2026 unverified in COMPARABLE\_RESULTS.md]}.

The wider literature describes three ways such numbers mislead. Single train--test splits are unstable in small
radiomics cohorts: in one brain tumour dataset, one random split tested at 0.667 and another at 0.911
\citep{an2021}. Performance drops on external data, by 0.10 or more for about a quarter of externally validated
deep learning algorithms \citep{yu2022}. And clinical comparators are rarely reported, although where they are,
they hold their own. Clinical variables with MGMT reached 0.69 for pseudoprogression, and adding radiomics made
the model worse \citep{leone2023}; a clinical model reached 0.727 externally against 0.717 for imaging alone
\citep{gomaa2025}; and in survival prediction clinical variables reached 0.74 against 0.75 with radiomics added
\citep{abler2025}.

A fourth problem has had little attention. In both of the best documented clinical comparators, time since the
end of RT was an input, and in one it was the most influential \citep{gomaa2025,leone2023}. A surveillance cohort
mixes scans taken before RT, during it, in the 12 weeks after it and in later follow-up, and event rates differ
sharply between these phases of care. A model that only recognises the phase can rank scans well whenever the
test set holds many easy phases. Routine tables add a related trap \citep{kapoor2023}. Treatment fields are
recorded once per patient, so if they are read as of the end of follow-up, a scan taken before RT ``knows'' about
the RT that follows it.

We built PRISM (Post-treatment Response Intelligence and Surveillance Monitor) for this task. It predicts
documented progression within 120 days from everything known on the scan day, explains each prediction and
writes a report for the clinician. Its first version, PRISM v1, scored AUC 0.80 on one split of 30 test
patients, which we call our initial evaluation split. Before building on that number we tested it, and each of
the problems above turned out to apply. This paper follows that test. We show how the initial split misled and
why. We then take PRISM apart branch by branch under repeated, nested cross-validation, with an external cohort
and a third cohort with diffusion imaging, and find that the phase of care, not MRI texture, carries the signal.
Finally we describe how PRISM was rebuilt around what remained: a modest core model whose every prediction comes
with an audit record and a report that can be checked against it.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:2}

\subsection{PRISM}\label{sec:2.1}

PRISM takes one MRI (T1, T1c, T2 and FLAIR with a four-compartment tumour mask) and the patient's clinical tables,
and returns the risk of documented progression within 120 days, an audit record and a clinician report
(Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). Every input is read as it stood on the scan day. Five branches can feed one ridge
logistic model: (1) clinical and molecular data; (2) the treatment course, from the time since diagnosis and RT
and the treatment status on the scan day; (3) imaging measurements (volumes, morphology and change); (4)
radiomics; and (5) a frozen SwinUNETR image encoder. Any branch can be switched off, which is how we ablate it.
An audit layer wraps the model and writes one record per scan, and a report generator turns each record into a
report (Sections~\ref{sec:2.7} and~\ref{sec:2.8}).

PRISM v1 differed in four ways. Its radiomics branch, which we call PRISM's initial radiomics model, was a
calibrated logistic regression on T1c and FLAIR radiomics plus clinical codes. Its image encoder was a small
transformer of the SwinUNETR type with untrained weights. A fusion network combined the encoder output with the
radiomics score. The report came from hard-coded RANO rules and a large language model (GPT-4.1-mini). When we
reproduced v1, the fusion network did not beat its radiomics input (AUC 0.8006 against 0.8043), the untrained
encoder carried no signal (about 0.50 alone), and the report's RANO measurements were derived from the model
score rather than from the masks (Supplementary S7). We replaced the encoder with pretrained frozen encoders and
the fusion network with one regularised model, read every input as of the scan day, and rebuilt the report on
the audit record.

\subsection{Data, outcome and analysis plans}\label{sec:2.2}

MU-Glioma-Post \citep{mahmoud2025} was the development cohort. It is a collection from a single centre of MRI
after surgery, with four-compartment masks (enhancing tumour, ET; non-enhancing core; surrounding FLAIR signal;
resection cavity) and a clinical workbook. The workbook gives the day of each scan, surgery, RT, chemotherapy,
first progression and death relative to diagnosis, and age, sex, grade, IDH and MGMT. LUMIERE \citep{suter2022}
was the external cohort: glioblastoma follow-up scans with an expert RANO rating, automatic segmentations, IDH and
MGMT, but no treatment dates. CFB-GBM \citep{cfbgbm} supported a diffusion analysis: 264 patients imaged at RT
planning and twice afterwards, with ADC, volumetric RANO 2.0 labels and released radiomics features, but no IDH or
MGMT. We used only its released tables.

An index scan is an MRI before first documented progression and before later lines of treatment, with all four
sequences, a mask and a known date. Each patient contributes every eligible scan, and every analysis keeps a
patient's scans together. Grade 4 glioma was the primary population, fixed in advance (Table~\ref{tab:1}; cohort
flow in Supplementary S2). The outcome is documented first progression within 120 days of the scan. A scan counts
as negative only if follow-up without progression reaches 120 days; otherwise it is censored, and death without
progression censors at the day of death. Discrimination is the AUC at 120 days weighted by the inverse probability
of censoring (IPCW AUC; \citealp{uno2007,blanche2013}). PRISM v1 counted censored scans as negatives; we use this
naive label only to reproduce its evaluation. In LUMIERE, progression is the first expert rating of progressive
disease.

Reporting follows TRIPOD+AI \citep{collins2024} and CLEAR (\citealp{kocak2023} \flag{[CHECK]}; Supplementary
S17). For each analysis we call prespecified, the plan and its decision rule were written into the analysis
document and code before any of its model outputs were seen; for the later analyses a dated copy of the plan was
saved before the run (Supplementary S1). None of these plans was registered externally. Everything else is
secondary or exploratory.

\subsection{Predictors}\label{sec:2.3}

All predictors are defined as of the scan day (Supplementary S4). Clinical: age, sex, IDH and MGMT, each with an
unknown category. Timing: days since diagnosis, days since RT ended, a flag for the 12 weeks after RT and its
interaction with MGMT methylation, and visit number. Treatment: whether RT and chemotherapy had started and whether
RT was complete, the prescription, and the days of surgery and RT start. A treatment fact enters only once its
documented start is on or before the scan day. Together these 23 predictors (31 model columns) form the
\emph{core model}. A compact alternative replaces timing and treatment with four phases of care: before RT,
during RT, within 12 weeks after RT, and later. Imaging measurements are compartment volumes, morphology and change
from earlier scans only. Radiomics are PRISM v1's T1c and FLAIR features and a corrected set covering all four
sequences (Supplementary S5). Deep features are pooled from frozen SwinUNETR encoders with BraTS 2021 or
BrainSegFounder weights (\citealp{cox2024} \flag{[CHECK]}), with untrained weights as a control (Supplementary
S6). Unknown values are imputed within each training fold with missingness indicators, and radiomics and deep
features are clipped to the training fold's 1st and 99th percentiles.

\subsection{Three ways to evaluate the same model}\label{sec:2.4}

We evaluated each model in three ways, from the least to the most reliable.

\textbf{The initial split.} Our initial evaluation split came from PRISM v1's own split search: for a given seed,
20,000 random draws of 30 test patients, keeping the draw closest to 96 test scans and 53 positives. Seed 62 gave
the split, and we reproduced the shipped model's stored test predictions to \num{1.1e-15}. With the final cohort
the split holds 126 training and 29 test patients (82 test scans). On it we fitted, once and without tuning, the
core model (ridge and gradient boosting), the core model plus legacy radiomics, and age, sex, IDH and MGMT alone.
Intervals come from 1,000 bootstrap draws over test patients.

\textbf{500 splits.} We reran v1's split search for seeds 0 to 499 and refitted four models on each split:
PRISM's initial radiomics pipeline with its feature ranking redone (the honest estimate), the same pipeline with
its 48 shipped features (optimistic, since those were chosen partly on other splits' test patients), and the core
model with and without radiomics. We report where our initial split ranks and how the AUC of each split relates to
the mix of phases in its test set (Supplementary S8).

\textbf{Repeated cross-validation.} All main estimates use one shared set of 5 $\times$ 5 repeated folds, grouped
by patient and stratified by outcome and grade. Every fitted step (imputation, clipping, scaling, feature
selection, stacking and hyperparameters) is fitted inside the training fold \citep{varma2006,cawley2010}. The
estimate is the mean over repeats of the pooled held-out IPCW AUC. 95\% CIs and paired differences come from
1,000 bootstrap draws over patients, with all models scored on the same draws.

\textbf{The treatment-date fix.} Our earlier runs read treatment fields as of the end of follow-up, so a scan
taken before RT carried the dose, start date and RT status of a course that had not yet begun (RT status was wrong
in 23 scans; Supplementary S4). We report results before and after the fix (Table~\ref{tab:2}).

\subsection{Ablation and phase of care}\label{sec:2.5}

Each branch is added to, or removed from, the core model. The primary learner is ridge logistic regression with
its penalty chosen by inner log loss; gradient boosting is secondary. Radiomics enters through 70 configurations
(feature set, stability filter, selection, and either concatenation or a stacked score) and deep features through
32, with 16 untrained controls. The honest estimate picks one configuration inside each outer training fold by
inner cross-validation (nested selection). With 70 to 80 uncorrected intervals, about two configurations are
expected to exclude 0 in each direction by chance. The prespecified primary development analysis compared the core model with
the core model plus radiomics (nested) in grade 4.

To test the phase of care, we built a ladder of bases without imaging (R0 clinical; R1 plus timing; R2 clinical
plus phase; R3 the core model) and added four imaging blocks to each (I1 volumes; I2 all imaging measurements
other than radiomics; I3 radiomics; I4 deep features). Models were trained and evaluated within the full cohort
and within the surveillance cohort, the scans taken after RT was complete (grade 4: 167 scans, 84 patients, 83
events). The prespecified primary contrast was R2 + I2 against R2 in grade 4 surveillance scans, with a 100-draw
permutation test that moves the imaging block between patients with the same number of scans. This plan was
written into the code before the full run but not committed separately (Supplementary S11).

\subsection{Giving imaging every chance}\label{sec:2.6}

When imaging added nothing, we tested four explanations, each with a rule fixed before its own run.

\textbf{The label.} Progressions documented on clinical grounds alone were censored, giving an imaging-defined
label. The label is the bottleneck if, in grade 4, imaging alone or the core model plus volumes and change beats
the core model by at least 0.03 with a 95\% CI excluding 0.

\textbf{The segmentation.} We checked volumes, labels and dates against their sources, then resegmented all 594
complete MU scans with the MONAI BraTS 2021 SwinUNETR and with BrainSegFounder. The new masks help if detection of
the progression scan itself rises by at least 0.05 with a CI excluding 0, or if ET is larger at progression than at
the previous scan in more than 65\% of pairs.

\textbf{The cohort.} Models trained on all MU scans were applied unchanged to LUMIERE. Without treatment dates the
transportable model was clinical variables, timing (days since surgery and a flag for the 3 months after RT) and
volumes, trained on grade 4 (Supplementary S13).

\textbf{The modality.} In CFB-GBM, with one row per patient, the outcome was volumetric RANO 2.0 progression
between the two scans after RT. Nested feature sets were clinical; plus tumour volume and its change; plus
conventional T1-Gd and FLAIR radiomics; plus ADC radiomics, evaluated by 20 repeats of 5-fold cross-validation.
ADC helps if it adds at least 0.03 with a CI excluding 0 (1,000-draw permutation test). We noted in advance that
the volume block is partly circular, because the label is defined by the same volume (Supplementary S18).

\subsection{The audit layer}\label{sec:2.7}

The audit layer wraps the deployed core model and adds no predictor (plan saved before the run; Supplementary
S15). It locks the model with a model card (code version, hashes of every input table, each input's definition,
source and as-of rule, training counts, intended and excluded uses) and writes one record per scan. The record
holds each input with its source and the day it became known; the phase of care; the risk with a 95\% interval from
200 bootstrap refits; the exact additive contribution of each input group to the log odds; a split conformal
prediction set at $\alpha$ = 0.2; and referral flags with reasons. A scan is flagged if it falls in the 12 weeks
after RT, if an input lies outside the training range or a molecular marker is unknown, or if its conformal set is
\{0, 1\}. The layer also tests selective prediction (does withholding the least confident scans help?), probes
shortcuts by refitting without the indicators for unknown markers and with treatment or timing alone, and measures
how stable the explanations are. Every fitted step, including the conformal calibration split (70/30 of training
patients), stays inside the shared training folds.

Two quantities were prespecified. \textbf{P1:} conformal coverage of at least 0.78 on test scans with a known
outcome. \textbf{P2:} the AUC on scans kept at about 70\% coverage, minus the AUC after removing the same number of
scans at random within each phase; met if its CI lies above 0.

\subsection{The report generator}\label{sec:2.8}

Each report is written from one scan's audit record and nothing else. Sections for scan context, inputs, risk
(interval, a descriptive tier and the conformal set), contributions, referral flags and volume change are rendered
directly from the record. A summary is written by a local language model (Qwen3.8-27B at a pinned revision, greedy
decoding, run offline on our own GPUs) that sees only the record's fields. Every number must be copied from a
field, every sentence must cite the fields it uses and every active flag must be stated; diagnoses, RANO
categories, management advice and unhedged certainty are forbidden. From v1 we kept the change table, the
explanation section and the tiered confidence language. We dropped the fixed alert threshold (set by hand on the
initial split), the RANO rule engine and the printed outcome label (Supplementary S19).

A deterministic checker scores every sentence. Cited fields must exist, and every number must match a cited value.
Every active flag must be mentioned, no inactive flag may be described as raised, the tier must match, and no
forbidden pattern may appear. Prompts and checker were developed on the 59 scans of other grades and frozen before
the evaluation on all 249 grade 4 scans. The prespecified primary quantity was the share of reports with no
unsupported claim and every active flag mentioned (target at least 0.95; Wilson 95\% CI). We also ran v1's prompt
on the same model and facts (arm B) and the grounded prompt on a smaller model (Qwen3.5-9B; arm C), and perturbed
30 records to check that the reports follow a changed risk or flag.

\section{Results}\label{sec:3}

\subsection{Cohorts}\label{sec:3.1}

MU-Glioma-Post contributed 308 index scans from 155 patients, of which 249 scans from 126 patients were grade 4
(Table~\ref{tab:1}). At 120 days, 100 scans were followed by documented progression, 156 were not and 52 were
censored (grade 4: 93, 117 and 39). Of the grade 4 scans, 76 were taken before RT, 6 during RT, 98 within 12 weeks
after RT and 69 later. LUMIERE contributed 132 scans from 61 patients (47 events, 76 without progression, 9
censored).

\subsection{The initial split scored high for every model}\label{sec:3.2}

Rescored with the final cohort and labels, our initial evaluation split was generous to every model
(Figure~\ref{fig:1}A; Table~\ref{tab:2}). PRISM's initial radiomics model reached 0.802 (IPCW 0.817, 95\% CI
0.690--0.920). The core model, which uses no imaging, reached 0.871 (IPCW 0.851, 0.735--0.947). Age, sex, IDH and
MGMT alone reached 0.791. The intervals were wide and overlapped.

One model went higher still. Before the treatment-date fix, gradient boosting on the core inputs scored 0.901 on
this split, yet under repeated cross-validation the same model averaged 0.723 in grade 4. Two things had lifted
it: treatment dates from the future, and the split. After the fix it scored 0.869 on the split, 0.844 with the
final labels, and 0.687 (0.601--0.768) under repeated cross-validation in grade 4. A model that is really about
0.70 had looked like 0.90.

\subsection{Why the split was easy}\label{sec:3.3}

The test set rewarded any model that recognises the phase of care. Of its 60 negative scans, 36 were taken before
RT, and none of those 36 was followed by documented progression, whereas 10 of the 68 scans before RT with a known
outcome in the whole cohort were (15\%; Figure~\ref{fig:1}B). So 60\% of the positive--negative pairs set a progression scan against a fresh
postoperative scan before RT. The core model ranked these pairs at 0.962 and the remaining pairs at 0.735. A rule
with no training at all, ``the scan was taken after RT'', scored 0.740 on this test set.

Across 500 splits of the same design (Figure~\ref{fig:2}A), PRISM's initial radiomics pipeline averaged 0.720
$\pm$ 0.055 when it reselected its features on each split. On our initial split it scored 0.801, above 92\% of
splits, and only 7\% of splits reached 0.802. Refitting the 48 shipped features gave 0.815 $\pm$ 0.044, an upper
bound inflated by selection on other splits' test patients. The core model averaged 0.801 $\pm$ 0.052, and our
split ranked it at the 91st percentile. The AUC of each split followed the mix of phases in its test set: for the
core model, its Spearman correlation with the AUC of the ``after RT'' rule was +0.57 (Figure~\ref{fig:2}B). From
composition alone, a regression predicted 0.851 for the core model on our split (observed 0.871, mean over splits
0.801). About two thirds of the split's excess came from its mix of scans, and the rest from its particular
patients.

\subsection{What carries the signal}\label{sec:3.4}

Under repeated cross-validation the picture was consistent and modest. In grade 4, clinical features alone
reached 0.585 (0.496--0.666), adding timing 0.644, and adding treatment status 0.698 (0.615--0.774): the core model
(Figure~\ref{fig:3}A). In all gliomas the same steps gave 0.619, 0.693 and 0.720 (0.638--0.792). Gradient boosting
did not beat ridge, and the core model was calibrated in the large (ratio of observed to expected events 0.96;
slope 0.89; Supplementary S12).

Most of this signal is the phase of care. Four phase categories recovered most of the gain over clinical features
(R2 0.664 against R0 0.585, a difference of +0.080, +0.005 to +0.152; Figure~\ref{fig:4}A), and treatment features
alone scored 0.701, as high as the full core model. Within the surveillance cohort, where the phase varies little,
no base model discriminated well (0.549 to 0.604), and the phase itself added nothing ($-$0.003).

No imaging branch showed a measurable gain (Table~\ref{tab:3}; Figure~\ref{fig:3}B). In the prespecified primary analysis,
nested radiomics changed the grade 4 core model by $-$0.032 ($-$0.084 to +0.017), and none of the 70
configurations had a CI above 0. In all gliomas radiomics changed it by +0.028 ($-$0.017 to +0.071). Frozen
pretrained encoders changed it by $-$0.004 ($-$0.069 to +0.063) in grade 4. Their features did carry an image
signal (0.652 alone, against 0.566 with untrained weights), but it tracked tumour volume (r = 0.58) and added
nothing to the core model. Radiomics also made the model overconfident (calibration slope 0.60) and lowered its net
benefit at a 30\% threshold by 0.020.

A model can give imaging credit without needing it. In models that included volumes, morphology and change, these
groups took about half of the SHAP credit (45\% with ridge, 51\% with boosting), yet the models scored no better
than the core model with the same learner (ridge 0.692 against 0.698; boosting 0.694 against 0.687;
Figure~\ref{fig:3}C).

\subsection{Imaging, given every chance}\label{sec:3.5}

\textbf{Within the surveillance cohort,} where the phase cannot do the work, the prespecified primary contrast
added all imaging measurements to clinical and phase variables. The AUC moved from 0.549 to 0.610, a difference of
+0.061 ($-$0.038 to +0.156; permutation p = 0.21; Figure~\ref{fig:4}B). The permutation null itself averaged
+0.028, because adding 44 columns of noise to a weak base raises its AUC. Radiomics gave the same +0.061 ($-$0.041
to +0.157). The hypothesis that imaging adds value after RT was not supported.

\textbf{The label was not the bottleneck.} Censoring the 12 of 93 grade 4 events that were documented on clinical
grounds alone gave the core model plus imaging 0.722 against 0.696, a difference of +0.027 ($-$0.033 to +0.082).
The rule was not met.

\textbf{Nor was the segmentation.} Our volumes reproduced the dataset's own table (r $\geq$ 0.996 for every
compartment), and labels and dates were aligned. Yet imaging separated the progression scan itself from verified
negatives with an AUC of only 0.68, and ET volume was larger at the progression scan than at the scan before in
only 49\% of 74 pairs. Resegmentation with the BraTS 2021 model agreed with the original masks (median Dice 0.80
for ET) and reduced the noise between closely spaced scans, but detection fell from 0.681 to 0.665, and ET was
larger at progression in 45\% of pairs. The rule was not met (Supplementary S14).

\textbf{The external cohort showed no imaging effect either.} On LUMIERE the locked model with volumes scored 0.608
(0.469--0.739) with ridge and 0.620 with boosting, against 0.667 and 0.675 in MU, while clinical variables and
timing alone scored 0.610 and 0.632. PRISM's initial radiomics model scored 0.613. The time to the next scan alone
scored 0.788, higher than any locked model, which shows how much this label depends on scheduling (Supplementary
S13).

\textbf{Diffusion did not change the result.} In 77 CFB-GBM patients with ADC (19 events), ADC radiomics added
+0.007 ($-$0.019 to +0.035; permutation p = 0.151) to conventional radiomics; the rule was not met. Tumour volume and
its change added 0.15 to 0.19, but that block is partly circular with the label, and conventional texture added
nothing beyond volume.

\subsection{Auditing a modest model}\label{sec:3.6}

The audit layer reproduced the core model's predictions exactly (largest difference \num{1.7e-16}), and the
contributions summed to every logit. \textbf{P1 was met:} conformal coverage on test scans with a known outcome was
0.841 (0.790--0.885) in grade 4.
Coverage failed where it mattered most, however. Almost every scan before RT received the set \{0\}, so all 5
grade 4 progressions before RT fell outside their sets.

\textbf{P2 was not met.} Withholding the least confident 30\% of scans gave the remaining scans an AUC of 0.731,
against 0.698 after random removal, but removing the same number of scans at random within each phase gave 0.725
(P2 +0.006, $-$0.023 to +0.036; Figure~\ref{fig:report}B). Abstention helped only by changing the phase mix: it
kept 97.6\% of scans taken before RT. Referral flags applied to 72.6\% of grade 4 scans.

\textbf{The audit's value was what it found.} First, a missingness shortcut: IDH was unknown in none of 93
progression scans but in 6 of 117 scans without progression, and removing the indicators for unknown markers
lowered the grade 4 AUC from 0.698 to 0.676 (difference 0.023, 0.001 to 0.047). Second, the core model is a treatment-course model: treatment
features alone scored 0.701. Third, a look-ahead: IDH and MGMT were used at 8 grade 4 scans taken 1 to 58 days
before the diagnosis date. Fourth, the same shortcut explained most of the miscalibration on LUMIERE, where IDH was
unknown in 40.9\% of scans. Without the indicators for unknown markers, the ratio of observed to expected events
there moved from 1.33 to 0.89. None of these problems showed in the cross-validated AUC.

\subsection{Reports that can be checked}\label{sec:3.7}

Of 249 grade 4 grounded reports, 236 (0.948; Wilson 95\% CI 0.913--0.969) had no unsupported claim and mentioned
every active flag, one report short of the 0.95 target. All 13 failures had the same cause: the risk sentence
stated the 120-day horizon without citing the horizon field, although the number was correct. No grounded report
omitted any of the 269 active flags, raised an inactive one or contained forbidden content
(Figure~\ref{fig:report}A).

The prompt mattered more than the model. Given the same facts, v1's prompt passed 171 of 249 reports against its
own input (0.687), and every one of its reports contained forbidden content: management advice in 99\%, a diagnosis
in 51\% and a RANO category in 41\%. It omitted 48 of the 269 active flags. The grounded prompt on the smaller
model passed 243 of 249 (0.976), with one real violation, a hedged diagnosis. The grounded reports followed 27 of
30 perturbed records. Reading 15 grounded reports against their records \todo{PENDING: name the reader(s)} found no
factual error (Supplementary S19).

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:4}

We built PRISM to predict progression from surveillance MRI, and its evaluation changed what PRISM is. Three
lessons emerged.

\textbf{A single split can measure the split.} Our initial 0.80 was real for that test set, but the test set was
easy: 60\% of its pairs set a progression scan against a fresh postoperative scan before RT, a contrast that any
model able to recognise the phase of care will win. The same split lifted the core model, which scores 0.698 under repeated
cross-validation, to 0.871, and lifted a boosting model with leaked treatment dates from 0.723 to 0.901. This adds a mechanism to the known instability of single splits \citep{an2021}. When phases with
very different event rates are mixed, the composition of a small test set explains much of the variance (about two
thirds of this split's excess), and a split search that matches the number of scans and positives does not
control it. We suggest four reporting rules for this task: repeated grouped cross-validation; the distribution over
splits whenever a single split is reported; results within phases of care; and a clinical comparator that knows the
treatment course.

\textbf{The phase of care is a confounder.} A scan before RT is rarely followed by documented progression within
120 days. A scan in the 12 weeks after RT often is, partly because of pseudoprogression and the follow-up it
triggers \citep{brandes2008,taal2008,wen2023}. Four phase categories recovered most of the core model's gain, and
treatment status alone matched the whole model. Two published clinical comparators also found time since RT
influential \citep{gomaa2025,leone2023}. Imaging models that pool scans from all phases can therefore report high
AUCs for recognising the phase rather than for tumour biology. This is why PRISM places every scan in its phase
and flags the 12 weeks after RT. The phase signal itself needs care, however: 63 of the 76 grade 4 scans before RT
came from 37 patients whose RT was never documented, and these patients account for 38 of the 39 censored grade 4
scans. Part of what the model learns as ``before RT'' may therefore be who stays in follow-up, not tumour
biology.

\textbf{Imaging, as tested here, showed no gain.} We found no pipeline defect, yet even at the scan where progression
was documented, volumes separated it from negatives with an AUC of only 0.68. Three features of the data explain
this. The label is a documentation event: one in eight grade 4 events was documented on clinical grounds alone,
and the 120-day label mostly means that the next scan was the progression scan. Volume series were noisy, with ET
changing as much over days as over months. And the cohort is small: by the Riley criteria even the 31-column core
model would need about ten times more scans \citep{riley2020}. Cleaning the label and resegmenting moved imaging
up by about 0.03, and imaging added 0.06 within the surveillance cohort, and the upper confidence bounds reach
0.02 to 0.16. Effects of that size cannot be excluded; our data cannot detect them. Diffusion did not change this in CFB-GBM, although that test was small.
Where imaging performs well in the literature, it uses diffusion and perfusion to classify a lesion that is
already suspicious \citep{kim2019,taylor2022}, a different question from forward risk at every scan. We keep
PRISM's imaging branches as optional modules so that larger cohorts can test them again. Attribution is no guide
here: imaging took half of the SHAP credit without improving discrimination, so a claim of imaging value needs a
paired difference with an interval.

\textbf{What PRISM offers.} At an AUC of about 0.70, PRISM does not offer better triage. Abstention and referral
raised the AUC of the remaining scans only by changing their phase mix, and the conformal sets met their target
mainly by calling every scan before RT negative. What PRISM does offer is a record of what the model knew on the
scan day, where the scan sits in the treatment course, how uncertain the estimate is and which inputs drove it.
Running the audit found a missingness shortcut, a molecular look-ahead and the cause of the external
miscalibration, none of which the AUC revealed. Grounding the language model in that record, and checking each
report against it, turned a report that issued RANO verdicts and management advice into one that restates the
record. We think this is the honest use of a modest model: to make its reasoning and its failure modes visible,
not to claim better decisions.

\textbf{Comparison with other work.} The grade 4 core model (0.698) is close to clinical comparators for
neighbouring tasks (0.69 in \citealp{leone2023}; 0.727 externally in \citealp{gomaa2025}) and to the nested result
for a volume label on the same dataset (0.728; \citealp{rai2026} \flag{[CHECK]}). It is below the single test set
result of 0.80 on MU-Glioma-Post \citep{christodoulou2026}, which used a different label (the progression status of
each scan) and which we did not re-evaluate. Higher AUCs for forward recurrence prediction elsewhere use different
cohorts, horizons and units, and some fall externally (0.887 to 0.762 in \citealp{chen2026}).

\subsection*{Limitations}

The development cohort comes from one centre, with 126 grade 4 patients and 93 events at 120 days, far below
recommended sample sizes. The outcome is documented progression from routine records, not a blinded RANO read, and
its timing depends on scheduling. MU images carry no acquisition metadata, so scanner effects could not be
studied. Censoring was concentrated before RT, in patients whose RT was never documented, while the IPCW weights
assume that censoring does not depend on the predictors; weights that depend on the phase, and a separate category
for undocumented RT, are still to be tested. Our bootstrap intervals resample fixed held-out predictions and do not
include the variability of refitting, so they are likely too narrow. Death was treated as censoring rather than as
a competing risk. The external cohort is small, uses a different segmenter and lacks treatment dates, so the full core model
was not tested externally. The label and segmentation tests were added after the imaging result was known,
although each had its rule fixed before its own run, and the plan for the phase ladder was not committed
separately. Radiomics used a fixed bin count rather than the fixed bin width recommended by IBSI
\citep{zwanenburg2020}, and the deep encoders were frozen rather than trained on this task. The look-ahead and the
missingness shortcut found by the audit also affect the reported core model, and we have not yet refitted without
them. The faithfulness checker is lexical: it verifies numbers, citations, flags and forbidden patterns but not
meaning, which we checked only by reading 15 reports. The reports come from one cohort, and no clinician has
assessed them.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

PRISM's initial AUC of 0.80 came from one favourable split. Across splits its radiomics pipeline averaged 0.72,
and its core of clinical and treatment-course variables reached about 0.70 under repeated cross-validation. MRI
radiomics, frozen deep features, resegmentation, a cleaner label and diffusion added no measurable discrimination.
The phase of care drives discrimination on this task and should be reported as a stratum and treated as a
confounder. PRISM is deployed as its core model, and every prediction is recorded, explained and reported in a
form that can be checked.
