\begin{abstract}
Machine learning models for glioma follow-up MRI report high accuracy, but a single AUC cannot show whether a model
reads the tumour or the calendar. We built PRISM, a local system that scores each follow-up scan separately from the
images and from the clinical history, states how certain each score is, and writes a report whose every number is
checked against its source. On MU-Glioma-Post, the time since radiotherapy and the enhancing tumour volume alone ranked
scans almost as well as the full model (AUC 0.80 against 0.82), and among scans taken at a similar time the images
ranked at 0.68. Timing did not carry over to other centres, but the model did: without retraining, it reached 0.77 at
LUMIERE and 0.86 at UCSF-ALPTDG. All 406 held-out reports passed the checks. Keeping the evidence apart shows what a
progression score rests on, and studies in this field should report it beside the AUC.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
glioma surveillance, clinical decision support, external validation
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:1}

After surgery and chemoradiotherapy for glioblastoma, patients are followed with MRI every few months, and each scan
feeds a decision: continue treatment, scan again sooner, or change treatment. The difficulty is that treatment can
mimic progression. Pseudoprogression follows chemoradiotherapy in 20--30\% of patients \citep{wen2010}, and in one
cohort scanned four weeks after radiotherapy, half of the scans that looked progressive were not \citep{taal2008}. It
is more frequent when the MGMT promoter is methylated \citep{brandes2008}. Clinicians therefore read each scan against
the time since treatment. RANO 2.0 takes the post-radiotherapy scan as the baseline and asks for a repeat scan or
histology before progression is called within 12 weeks of radiotherapy \citep{wen2023}. Practice still varies: of 220
European centres, 60\% reported follow-up scans in free text and 27\% applied RANO \citep{thust2018}. Structured
reporting systems such as BT-RADS make the treatment history part of the report \citep{weinberg2018}, and after its
adoption reports more often recorded treatment and MGMT status \citep{zhang2020} and were rated more consistent by
referring clinicians \citep{gore2019}.

Advanced imaging has not settled the question. Across 30 studies, diffusion and perfusion MRI reached pooled AUCs
near 0.9 for separating pseudoprogression from progression \citep{taylor2022}, and adding them to a radiomics model
raised its AUC in 61 patients \citep{kim2019}. In another cohort of 105 patients, however, they added nothing to a
model of clinical data and MGMT status, and radiomics and deep learning models performed near chance
\citep{leone2023}.

Machine learning models proposed for this decision mostly score the image alone and report one AUC from a single
split of one cohort. Studies of treatment response have small samples and a high risk of bias \citep{booth2022}, only
29\% of 51 glioma radiomics studies were validated externally \citep{park2020}, and in a review of automated response
assessment, 3 of 20 studies considered clinical characteristics and 5 used external test data \citep{shi2025}. Single
random splits of small radiomics cohorts give unstable estimates \citep{an2021}, and most radiology algorithms lose
performance on external data \citep{yu2022}. On the public MU-Glioma-Post cohort, a radiomics model reported an AUC of
0.80 on 30 held-out patients \citep{christodoulou2026}. Where clinical inputs were compared with images on external
data, the time since treatment was the strongest single input \citep{gomaa2025}. Longitudinal and interpretable
designs have begun to appear: a temporal model of serial scans predicted recurrence of paediatric glioma across
institutions \citep{tak2025}, and a concept bottleneck model on LUMIERE passes predicted tumour measurements through the
RANO rules, although the interval between scans strongly affected its output \citep{tarek2026}.

A single AUC cannot show what the ranking rests on. Models learn shortcuts, signals that predict the label in the
development data without being what the reader cares about \citep{geirhos2020}: image models have recognised the
hospital that acquired a radiograph \citep{zech2018} and predicted hip fracture largely from patient and healthcare
variables \citep{badgeley2019}. In follow-up imaging the schedule ties the time of a scan to its outcome, so a score
can rank scans well by recognising when they were taken. The useful question is what a model adds beyond the evidence
a clinician already holds \citep{pencina2008,beaulieujones2021}. Heat maps drawn over a score after the fact do not
answer it reliably: on chest radiographs every saliency method tested failed at least one test of trustworthiness
\citep{arun2021}, and all localised findings less accurately than radiologists \citep{saporta2022}. For high-stakes
decisions, a model whose inputs can be read directly is preferable \citep{rudin2019}.

Image measurements depend on how the tumour is outlined. After surgery and radiotherapy the scan contains a resection
cavity and treatment changes, which make segmentation harder than before surgery, and the BraTS 2024 challenge was
set up for this setting \citep{deverdier2024}. Experts disagree about the quality of the same glioma
segmentation \citep{hoebel2024}, so a volume measured from one set of outlines may not match another, even between
scans a few weeks apart.

The report is the last step, and it should be honest about uncertainty and checkable. Conformal prediction turns a
score into a set of possible outcomes that contains the true one at a chosen rate without assumptions about the
model \citep{angelopoulos2023}, although in medical image classification the guarantee fails within subgroups and
under distribution shift \citep{mehrtens2023}. Language models now draft reports from automated measurements of brain
tumours \citep{valerio2025,herasrivera2026} and other lesions \citep{mansour2026}, but they make errors a reader may
not catch. AI-written radiology reports contain clinically significant errors \citep{tanno2025}, 6\% of oncologic CT
reports simplified by an open-weight model contained factual errors in a prospective trial \citep{prucker2025}, and
systems that write text from tables have long misstated the numbers they were given \citep{wiseman2017}. Automatic
measures of factual accuracy disagree with one another \citep{gabriel2021}, so checkers for clinical text test each
statement against the record \citep{zeba2026}, and checkers for radiology reports are tested on planted errors
\citep{rao2025rexerr}. Because patient records often cannot leave the hospital, the model should also run locally, and
open-weight models run on site now read radiology reports as well as closed ones \citep{mukherjee2023,nowak2025}.

We present PRISM, a local system for glioma follow-up MRI built around these needs. PRISM works in six stages
(Figure~\ref{fig:1}). It first outlines the enhancing tumour, necrosis, FLAIR hyperintensity and resection cavity
with a fixed public segmentation network, either a Swin UNETR trained on BraTS 2021 \citep{hatamizadeh2022} or the
first-placed BraTS 2024 post-treatment model, or it takes the masks supplied with the dataset. From the outlines of
the current and earlier scans it computes 27 measurements: volumes, shape, intensity and change. Three penalised
logistic regression models \citep{pedregosa2011} score the scan, one on the image measurements, one on the clinical
history and one on both; the imaging model is never given dates. Beside each score PRISM states an 80\% conformal set,
whether the imaging and clinical-history scores agree, and how the models performed in comparable scans. Every value
is collated into one structured record under a fixed key, with its source. An open-weight language model
(Qwen3.8-27B, served locally with vLLM \citep{kwon2023}) writes the report from that record alone, under a JSON schema
that makes each sentence name the record entries it uses. It sees no images, has no tools and decides nothing. Two
rule-based programs then check every number and every direction in the report against the record and accept or
reject it. Related systems use a language model to phrase automated measurements \citep{herasrivera2026}, or chain
language-model agents that read clinical notes and assign a BT-RADS category \citep{jabal2026}. In PRISM the clinical
history enters the record as data, which is what makes every sentence checkable.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A system that keeps the evidence apart.} For each follow-up scan, PRISM scores the images, the clinical
history and both separately, states how certain each score is, and writes a report from one record that holds every
value with its source.
\item \textbf{A way to see what a score rests on.} We compare each score with a simple model of the time since
radiotherapy and the enhancing tumour volume, and split its AUC into scans taken at a similar time and scans taken at
different times. On MU-Glioma-Post this showed that timing and the enhancing tumour carry much of the ranking, and
that the images rank scans at about 0.68 within a time window.
\item \textbf{Transfer without retraining.} The models were fitted once on MU-Glioma-Post and applied unchanged at
LUMIERE and UCSF-ALPTDG, from the supplied masks and from PRISM's own segmentation.
\item \textbf{A report that is checked.} Every number and direction in the report is tested against the record by
rule-based programs, and we measure what these checks catch and what they miss.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the paper follows the system from data to report. Section~\ref{sec:2} describes the cohorts and the
label, PRISM's stages (segmentation and measurements, the three models, the confidence statements, and the record,
report and checks), and how we evaluated it, including the time-and-enhancement model, the AUC split by time window,
the published radiomics pipeline, the segmentation comparison and the tests fixed in advance. Section~\ref{sec:3}
reports how the models ranked scans at the three centres, what that ranking rests on, how it changes with the
segmentation, how often the conformal sets held, and what the report checks caught. Section~\ref{sec:4} discusses
what these results mean for reading follow-up scans and for evaluating models in this setting, and states the
limitations.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:2}

\subsection{Cohorts and label}\label{sec:2.1}

% Sources: TABLE1_COHORT.md; UCSF_DATA.md; UCSF_PLAN.md; DETECTION_TASK.md; INTENDED_USE.md; NUMBERS.md 2.2.
We developed PRISM on MU-Glioma-Post \citep{mahmoud2025} and tested it without retraining on LUMIERE
\citep{suter2022} and UCSF-ALPTDG \citep{fields2024}, with the tests and their rules fixed in advance
(Table~\ref{tab:1}, Table~\ref{tab:2}). Grade 4 glioma was the primary population in all three cohorts. The task was
to tell the scan at which progression was documented from scans without progression. At MU-Glioma-Post, the scan with
progression is the first scan on or after the progression date in the clinical record, and a scan without progression
must be followed by at least 120 days without progression. LUMIERE rates every scan, so the scan with progression is
the first one rated as progressive disease, and the scans without progression are those rated stable or responding
before it. UCSF-ALPTDG supplies two consecutive scans per patient and a progression date. We evaluated the second scan,
labelled as progression when progression was dated within 30 days of it and as no progression when it was followed by
at least 120 days without progression (Table~\ref{tab:eval}). Because the three centres define progression
differently, we compared models with one another within each cohort.

% Sources: DETECTION_TASK.md; NUMBERS.md 2.2 and mismatch 7.
At MU-Glioma-Post the intended use covers scans on or after a documented start of radiotherapy. The evaluation sets
held 146 scans from 80 patients at MU-Glioma-Post, drawn from 181 detection scans of 92 patients among the cohort's
126 grade 4 patients, 178 scans from 79 patients at LUMIERE and 69 scans from 69 patients at UCSF-ALPTDG. The 120-day
rule leaves out scans whose follow-up was shorter, so on MU we also evaluated every score with all scans before
progression as scans without progression, as the detection plan specified. The label is tied to time after
radiotherapy. At MU, most scans with progression were taken later than 12 weeks after radiotherapy and most scans
without progression within 12 weeks (Table~\ref{tab:1}, Figure~\ref{fig:cohorts}), so the time window alone separates
many pairs of scans.

\paragraph*{Segmentation and measurements.} PRISM outlines the tumour with a fixed public network, or uses the masks
supplied with each dataset, and computes 27 measurements of the tumour and its change. The masks supplied with each
dataset (the supplied masks) are automatic masks at MU-Glioma-Post, DeepBraTumIA masks with three labels at LUMIERE
and the masks provided with UCSF-ALPTDG (Table~\ref{tab:1}). The primary results use them, so every volume can be
traced to a public file. PRISM's own segmentation stage holds two networks, each run unchanged with its public weights
and never tuned on our cohorts. The first is a Swin UNETR trained on BraTS 2021 \citep{hatamizadeh2022}
(Figure~\ref{fig:1}b). It takes FLAIR, T1c, T1 and T2 as four channels, each normalised over its non-zero voxels,
slides a window of $128^3$ voxels across the brain with half overlap, and thresholds its three sigmoid outputs (tumour
core, whole tumour and enhancing tumour) at 0.5. The enhancing tumour is taken directly, necrosis is the core less the
enhancing tumour, and FLAIR hyperintensity is the whole tumour less the core. This network was trained on scans before
surgery and does not outline the resection cavity, so it supplies 22 of the 27 measurements. The second is the
first-placed model of the BraTS 2024 challenge on post-treatment adult glioma \citep{deverdier2024,ferreira2024}, an
ensemble of nnU-Net and MedNeXt networks that labels the resection cavity as well and so supplies all 27. LUMIERE's
images are stored on an atlas grid with a different axis orientation, so before either network they are reoriented to
the orientation of the training data, and the label map is mapped back. This step is the same for every LUMIERE scan.

From the masks and images, PRISM computes 27 measurements. Nineteen describe the current scan: the log volumes of the
enhancing tumour, necrosis, FLAIR hyperintensity, resection cavity, tumour core and whole lesion, and 13 measures of
shape and of T1 contrast and FLAIR intensity relative to normal appearing brain (Supplementary Table~S7). Eight
describe change since the previous scan and since the patient's smallest volume. Five measurements depend on the
cavity (its volume, whether there is one, the share of enhancing tumour next to it, the distance of the enhancing
tumour from it, and its change since the previous scan).

PRISM also takes the treatment course as of the scan day: days since diagnosis and surgery, radiotherapy start, end
and dose, and whether chemotherapy had started. It places the scan in a time window after radiotherapy (before its
end, within 12 weeks after its end, later, or unknown when dates are missing), and it takes age, sex, and IDH mutation
and MGMT promoter methylation status. Every input is taken as of the scan day. IDH and MGMT status count as unknown at
scans before the diagnosis date (8 grade 4 scans, used only in training). The duration of radiotherapy and the time
since its end enter only after radiotherapy has ended, and the prescribed dose enters from its start.

\paragraph*{Three models.} Three logistic regression models score each scan: one on the images, one on the clinical
history and one on both. The imaging model uses the 27 measurements and is never given dates. The clinical-history
model uses the treatment course and the patient's characteristics, and the combined model uses both input sets.
PRISM shows all three, so the record shows which kind of evidence a score follows. Each model is a logistic regression
with an L2 (ridge) penalty \citep{pedregosa2011}, chosen because it is simple and uses few inputs. Deep networks tuned
on MU did not improve on it (Table~\ref{tab:2}). Inputs are standardised, and the penalty is chosen from six values of
the inverse strength $C$ (0.01 to 3) by threefold cross validation grouped by patient, minimising log loss.

LUMIERE records only the days since surgery, and UCSF-ALPTDG the days since surgery and the start of radiotherapy,
so the full
clinical-history model cannot be computed there. For these centres PRISM uses reduced models, fitted on MU with the
inputs that every cohort records. The reduced clinical-history model takes age, sex, IDH and MGMT status, days since
surgery and a flag for the first weeks after radiotherapy. At LUMIERE the flag is the rater's mark for scans within
three months of radiotherapy, and at UCSF-ALPTDG it is approximated from the start of radiotherapy, assuming a six
week course. The reduced combined model adds the enhancing, necrosis (with cavity) and FLAIR volumes, their change
since the previous scan, and the interval since that scan. LUMIERE's masks have three compartments, so its imaging
model is fitted on MU with those three volumes and their change. Because the imaging model needs only scans and masks,
PRISM gives an imaging score at a centre that records no treatment dates.

% Sources: prism/audit/conformal.py; METHODS_PLAN.md M2; RELIABILITY_CARD.md; REVISION_DATA.md R2; refs
% angelopoulos2023, vovk2022book, romano2020, barber2021limits, johansson2015, ding2023, mehrtens2023, mitchell2019.
\paragraph*{Confidence statements.} Each score comes with an 80\% set of possible outcomes, a statement of whether the
imaging and clinical-history scores agree, and the measured performance of the models in comparable scans. A conformal
set names the outcomes a score cannot rule out \citep{angelopoulos2023}. Write $\hat p(1) = \hat p$ for a scan's score
and $\hat p(0) = 1 - \hat p$. For each calibration scan $i$ with outcome $y_i$ the nonconformity score is
$s_i = 1 - \hat p_i(y_i)$. Because a patient contributes several scans, each calibration scan is weighted by one over
the number of that patient's calibration scans. With $\alpha = 0.2$ and $n$ calibration patients, the threshold $\hat
q$ is the smallest $s_i$ whose cumulative weight reaches $(1 - \alpha)(n + 1)$, and a new scan receives
\begin{equation}
C(\hat p) = \{\, y \in \{0, 1\} : 1 - \hat p(y) \le \hat q \,\}.
\end{equation}
As planned, each model was refitted on 70\% of the training patients and calibrated on the other 30\%, inside each
training fold on MU and once on all MU training patients for the external cohorts. The combined score's set is the
one evaluated as primary. If a new patient is exchangeable with the calibration patients, about 80\% of their sets
contain the true outcome, on average over scans but not necessarily within a subgroup
\citep{romano2020,barber2021limits}. The weighting by patient makes even the average coverage approximate, and
coverage carries over to an external cohort only if its scans are exchangeable with MU's \citep{mehrtens2023}.

% Sources: METHODS_PLAN.md M2; Supplementary Section S10.
Group-conditional (Mondrian) sets \citep{vovk2022book} compute $\hat q$ separately within each group of comparable
scans. Our groups cross the time window with the state of the enhancing tumour (absent below 0.1\,cc, without
comparison when there is no previous scan, increased when it grew by at least 1\,cc and 40\%, or not increased). Most
groups are small, so each scan is calibrated at the finest of three levels that holds at least 10 calibration
patients (its group, then its enhancement state over all windows, then all calibration scans), a fixed fallback of the
kind used for small Mondrian groups \citep{johansson2015,ding2023}. Because group calibration can raise coverage in a
group simply by making sets larger, we compared it after the planned run with marginal sets at the $\alpha$ that gives
the same share of sets holding both outcomes (exploratory).

The record carries two further descriptive statements. The agreement statement says whether the clinical-history and
imaging scores fall on the same side of their cohort medians. The reliability card, after model cards
\citep{mitchell2019}, gives for the scan's group how many scans with and without progression the models were tested
on and, with at least 10 of each (the tested tier), their AUCs, and otherwise only the counts (few cases). The card
counts the cohort's tested scans. Recomputed without the patient's own scans, it changed the tier of one MU scan.

% Sources: prism/report/facts_v3_1.py; prism/report/prompts_v3_1.py; prism/report/checker.py; prism/report/verifier.py
% (v1.1); REPORT_V3.md; REPORT_V3_1.md; METHODS_PLAN.md M3; METHODS_RESULTS.md M3; Supplementary S8; refs zeba2026,
% wiseman2017, gabriel2021, rao2025rexerr, weinberg2018, kwon2023.
\paragraph*{Record, report and checks.} All values go into one record with their sources. A local language model
writes the report from that record alone, and two rule-based programs check every number and every direction against
it. Every score, measurement and statement sits in the record under a fixed key. The language model, Qwen3.8-27B
served with vLLM \citep{kwon2023} under greedy decoding, runs on the computer that runs PRISM, so the record never
leaves it. Decoding is constrained to a JSON schema in which every sentence carries its section and the keys of the
record fields it uses. The model sees no images, has no tools and does no arithmetic. The report page follows the
content of the BT-RADS follow-up template \citep{weinberg2018}, with the measurements in tables and a short narrative,
and without the template's category or advice, which PRISM does not give (Supplementary Section~S8). Because the
supplied volumes are noisy (Section~\ref{sec:3.3}), the report calls them indicative.

The checker, frozen before any evaluation report was generated, rejects a report that cannot be parsed, cites a field
that does not exist, states a number found in no cited field, omits a raised flag, misstates the conformal set or the
agreement, omits the card's tier where evidence is thin, leaves out a score, quotes a performance figure absent from
the card, or offers a diagnosis, a response category, advice or words of certainty. A rejected report is not
repaired. Such rule-based checks compare generated text with a source record \citep{zeba2026}, as extractive
evaluations of data-to-text systems do with a learned extractor \citep{wiseman2017}. Every LUMIERE report failed the
checker at the first evaluation, because a sentence about the rater's three-month flag stated a number held in no
field. We revised the record fields and the prompt once, on development patients, and generated the evaluation
reports again. Because the checker accepts a number that equals a number in any cited field, it accepts a sentence
that gives the FLAIR volume as the enhancing volume while citing both fields, or that writes ``larger'' where the
record says ``smaller''.

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md M3; METHODS_PLAN.md M3; CHECKER_MUTATION.md.
The verifier closes these gaps with two rules that bind a claim to what it refers to. A sentence is split into clauses
at commas, semicolons, colons and the word ``and'', and each number and direction word in a clause is assigned to the
nearest tumour region or score named in it. The first rule requires every such number to equal, up to its written
rounding, a cited value of that region or score. The second requires every direction word on a fixed list (larger,
increased, grew, rose, higher, and their opposites) to agree with the cited direction of its region. As first
written, the rules read the region only from the names of cited fields and rejected every evaluation report, because
most fields are sentences such as ``enhancing tumour 3.5\,cc larger than at the previous scan''. We let the rules read
the region from the text of a cited field as well, tested this change only on synthetic records and the 48 MU and
LUMIERE reports used to develop the prompt, froze it, and ran it again on the evaluation reports. To measure the
verifier we planted one error at a time in correct reports, with 24 kinds of error, as error injection is used to
evaluate factuality checks \citep{gabriel2021,rao2025rexerr}. Three binding errors were held out of rule writing.
Because we wrote both the rules and these errors, a separate language model that had not seen the rules then wrote
further errors in its own words, with correct paraphrases as controls (exploratory). Both programs test whether a
report agrees with its record. Neither tests whether the record, or the report, is clinically correct.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:2.3}

% Sources: DETECTION_TASK.md; UCSF_PLAN.md; SYSTEM_PLAN.md; METHODS_PLAN.md; refs varoquaux2018.
\paragraph*{Development and testing without retraining.} Each cohort played one role (Table~\ref{tab:eval}). On MU we
evaluated every model by five repeats of fivefold cross validation grouped by patient and report the mean pooled
held-out AUC, because single splits are unstable in small cohorts \citep{varoquaux2018}. We then fitted each model
once on all 181 grade 4 detection scans of MU, including the 35 without a documented start of radiotherapy, and
applied it unchanged to LUMIERE and UCSF-ALPTDG. At UCSF-ALPTDG every model was planned before any score was compared
with the label. At LUMIERE the combined model was planned and the other scores are exploratory.

% Sources: CLINICIAN_BASELINE_PLAN.md; METHODS_RESULTS.md E4; ref beaulieujones2021.
\paragraph*{The time-and-enhancement model.} We judged each score against a simple model of the time since
radiotherapy and the enhancing tumour volume, which measures what a score adds to evidence a clinician already holds
\citep{beaulieujones2021}. It is a ridge logistic regression on the time window after radiotherapy, the log enhancing
volume and its change since the previous scan, fitted with the same pipeline and folds as PRISM's models and applied
unchanged in the same way. At LUMIERE the window is the rater's flag. The plan fixed the reading in advance. A gain
whose 95\% CI lies above zero counts as a gain, and one whose CI includes zero is reported as no clear gain. To see how
far time alone goes, we also scored scans by timing alone (exploratory, Table~\ref{tab:models}): the days since the
end of radiotherapy and the visit number without any fitted model, and a ridge model on every timestamp input of the
clinical-history model.

% Sources: METHODS_PLAN.md M1; prism/analyses/methods.py; refs kallus2019, janes2008, janes2009.
\paragraph*{Splitting the AUC by time window.} We split each score's AUC into pairs of scans taken at a similar time
and pairs taken at different times. Let $P$ and $N$ be the scans with and without progression, $s$ a score, and $c$
the time window of each scan. The AUC is the share of pairs ranked correctly,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{1}{|P|\,|N|} \sum_{i \in P} \sum_{j \in N} K(s_i, s_j), \qquad
K(a, b) = \mathbb{1}[a > b] + \tfrac12\, \mathbb{1}[a = b].
\end{equation}
The pairs split into $W = \{(i, j) : c_i = c_j\}$, two scans from the same window, and its complement $B$. With
$A_W$ and $A_B$ the same average over $W$ and over $B$, and $w = |W| / (|P|\,|N|)$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{AUC} = w\, A_W + (1 - w)\, A_B
\end{equation}
holds exactly \citep{kallus2019}. $A_W$ is the ranking with the window held fixed, a pair weighted analogue of the
covariate adjusted AUC \citep{janes2008,janes2009}. A score with $A_B$ above $A_W$ ranks pairs across windows better
than pairs within one, which can come from the window or from real differences between the tumours of different
windows. To show how much of $A_B$ the window alone could supply, we also scored every scan by the progression rate of
its window in the training folds (the window only score). At MU the window comes from the treatment dates (during
radiotherapy, within 12 weeks after its end, and later). At LUMIERE it is the rater's flag for scans within three
months of radiotherapy, and at UCSF-ALPTDG it is computed from the start of radiotherapy, assuming a six week course.
Externally, the window only score takes MU's rate for the matching window. The identity was checked on every
bootstrap sample.

% Sources: CHRISTODOULOU_FAITHFUL_PLAN.md (with deviations); CHRISTODOULOU_REPLICATION.md (faithful run);
% ref christodoulou2026.
\paragraph*{The published radiomics pipeline.} We ran the published MU radiomics pipeline \citep{christodoulou2026}
with the authors' public code and settings, on the same patients, masks, label and folds as PRISM. It extracts 4,892
radiomic features from the original, Laplacian of Gaussian and wavelet images of the four sequences of the current
scan, keeps 256 of them within each training set by the authors' ranking, and fits a class weighted gradient boosted
model tuned over 400 Bayesian search trials with patient grouped inner folds, followed by Platt calibration. The
authors' ranking adds permutation importance only when an inner fold holds at least 30 scans, so with our 13 to 16
scans it ranked features by coefficient size alone. We left the code unchanged. The published study gave every scan
of a patient who ever progressed the label of progression. We kept PRISM's label, one scan per documented
progression, so the comparison is of methods on one task, not a replication of the published estimate. The plan,
committed before the run, stated that the comparison with PRISM's imaging model would be withdrawn if the pipeline
did not rank below it.

% Sources: MASK_SENSITIVITY_PLAN.md; METHODS_RESULTS.md E5; SEGMENTER_STAGE_PLAN.md; SEGMENTER_STAGE.md;
% SEGMENTER_BRATS24_PLAN.md; CHRISTODOULOU_BRATS_MASKS_PLAN.md.
\paragraph*{Segmentation comparison.} We measured how each segmenter changes the results, in three planned analyses.
First, on MU we replaced every supplied mask with the Swin UNETR label map, kept the resection cavity of the supplied
mask, and refitted the imaging and combined models, the time-and-enhancement model and the published radiomics
pipeline in the same folds. Second, we ran PRISM from Swin UNETR alone at all three centres, with its 22 measurements
and nothing taken from the supplied masks, refitting on MU and applying the models unchanged externally. This plan was
written before any segmenter-derived number was linked to a label at LUMIERE or UCSF-ALPTDG. At UCSF-ALPTDG the
time-and-enhancement model was applied here with the approximated window. Third, the BraTS 2024 model replaces Swin
UNETR in the second analysis, with all 27 measurements. Each analysis compares each score with its value on the
supplied masks by paired AUC differences, and the rule asked that every paired CI include zero or lie above it, and
that the reading of the combined model's gain over the time-and-enhancement model be the same under both mask sets.
We also report agreement with the supplied masks (Dice) and the repeatability of the enhancing volume, as the median
fold change between consecutive scans at most 30 days apart, for which a single segmenter applied to every scan was
expected to be more stable.

\subsection{What the ranking rests on}\label{sec:3.2}

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md E4; NUMBERS.md 3 (single inputs, B0, P - B1); SKELETON_2026-11-20.md timestamp-only check
% (exploratory, 2026-11-20); DETECTION_DEEP.md.
At MU, the time since the end of radiotherapy alone, without any fitted model, ranked scans at 0.77, and the
time-and-enhancement model at 0.80 (0.71--0.88), so the combined model added little there: $+$0.02 ($-$0.03 to
$+$0.08). These timing scores are exploratory. The visit number alone reached 0.78, and a ridge model on every
timestamp reached 0.76 (0.66--0.84). The time since radiotherapy is a legitimate clinical input, which clinicians
already weigh. At MU it carries much of the label, because 56 of the 62 scans with progression were taken later than
12 weeks after radiotherapy and 51 of the 84 scans without progression within 12 weeks, when clinicians ask for
confirmation before they call progression. The patient's characteristics
alone, without dates, reached 0.53 (0.45--0.61), so the clinical-history model's ranking comes from the dates. The
planned test of whether image measurements and schedule inputs add to the clinical-history model was not met, and
deep networks tuned on MU did not add to it either (Table~\ref{tab:2}). At LUMIERE, the time-and-enhancement model,
applied unchanged, reached 0.73 (0.64--0.81), and the combined model ranked scans above it by 0.04 ($-$0.01 to
$+$0.10), again with no clear gain.

% Sources: SKELETON_2026-11-20.md timestamp-only check (LUMIERE days since surgery, UCSF days from RT start, UCSF days
% since previous scan); UCSF_AUDIT.md sections 3--6; UCSF_RESULTS.md Part C; NUMBERS.md 4, 5, 12C; SEGMENTER_STAGE.md P1.
Timing did not carry over to the other centres, yet the combined model held, because there the enhancing tumour did
more of the work. The days since surgery ranked LUMIERE scans at 0.61, and the days since the start of radiotherapy
ranked UCSF-ALPTDG scans at 0.54 (both exploratory). At UCSF-ALPTDG, enhancing volume alone reached 0.88 (0.79--0.95),
and the time-and-enhancement model, applied with the approximated window in the segmentation comparison, 0.87. The
label there follows enhancement. UCSF-ALPTDG rated enhancement as increased at 27 of the 29 scans with progression
and dated progression a median of 7 days after the scan (exploratory). Scans with progression had also been brought
forward. The days since the previous scan ranked them at 0.28, that is 0.72 with shorter intervals ranked as
progression, and the reduced combined model holds this interval as an input. LUMIERE's label is an expert's rating
of the scan itself, and there enhancing volume alone reached 0.66 (exploratory).

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md M1, E1, E4 and UCSF section; SKELETON_2026-11-20.md (all timestamps within window).
Among scans taken at a similar time after radiotherapy, the images ranked progression at about 0.68
(Figure~\ref{fig:3}). At MU, 38\% of pairs fell within one window. The imaging model ranked 0.68 (0.58--0.77) of the
pairs within a window correctly and 0.73 across windows, a difference whose interval includes zero. The ridge model on
timestamps ranked pairs within a window at 0.49 (0.37--0.60), at chance. The clinical-history model ranked 0.93
(0.88--0.97) of the pairs across windows correctly and 0.64 (0.51--0.78) of the pairs within one, and ranking scans by
their window's progression rate alone also ranked 0.93 of the pairs across windows correctly. The clinical-history
score therefore ranks mainly across windows, and the combined model followed it (0.65 within, 0.92 across). Adding
clinical history to images raised the AUC by 0.11 (0.03--0.19) at MU and 0.12 (0.04--0.21) at LUMIERE, and at both
centres the whole gain lay in pairs across windows. Within one window, the imaging model ranked scans above the
time-and-enhancement model by 0.10 ($-$0.04 to $+$0.26) at MU. Nine in ten pairs within a window come from the later
window, so the within window AUC is close to the AUC of scans taken later than 12 weeks after radiotherapy
(exploratory, Supplementary Section~S10).

% Sources: METHODS_RESULTS.md M1 (LUMIERE, UCSF).
At LUMIERE the window is the rater's flag, and only one of the scans the rater flagged as within three months of
radiotherapy had progression (Table~\ref{tab:1}). The reduced models take the flag as an input, so the reduced
clinical-history model's across window AUC of 0.96, against 0.59 within one, shows mainly how much the flag carries.
There the imaging model ranked scans better within a window (0.71) than across (0.51). At UCSF-ALPTDG nearly nine in
ten pairs fell within one window, almost all later than 12 weeks after radiotherapy, so the within window AUCs
(combined 0.87, imaging 0.73) are close to the overall ones and the pairs across windows are too few to read.

% Sources: STRATIFIED_OFFSET.md Q1, Q2; IMAGING_S3.md (exploratory design analyses, 2026-11-20).
No change to the imaging model raised its ranking within a window (exploratory). An imaging model fitted with a

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:4}

% Sources: Sections 3.1--3.3; refs booth2022, shi2025, christodoulou2026, gomaa2025.
Keeping the scores separate showed where a progression model's ranking comes from: the time since radiotherapy at the
development centre, the enhancing tumour elsewhere, and about 0.68 from the images within a time window. A single AUC
measures how well a score ranks scans, and the separate scores show what that ranking rests on. At MU-Glioma-Post the
clinical-history and combined models ranked scans well because the cohort's follow-up schedule ties the time of a scan
to its label, and the time since treatment was the strongest input in an earlier external comparison
\citep{gomaa2025}. The time since radiotherapy is information a clinician already uses, and PRISM keeps it in its own
score so that the reader can see how much of a combined score it supplies. At LUMIERE and UCSF-ALPTDG timing alone ranked
scans far less well, and the combined model kept its ranking because the enhancing volume and its change
carried the signal there. Most models proposed for this decision score the image alone and are tested on one split
\citep{booth2022,shi2025}. On the same data, the published radiomics pipeline ranked scans below PRISM's 27
measurements, and its selected features ranked no better than the enhancing volume, which suggests that in cohorts of
this size a few measurements with a known meaning hold the image signal that thousands of features dilute.

% Sources: Section 3.3; METHODS_RESULTS.md E5; refs hoebel2024, deverdier2024.
The same separation explains why the combined score survived a change of segmenter while the imaging score did not.
The combined and time-and-enhancement models rest on the time window and the enhancing volume, and the enhancing
volume ranks scans similarly whichever network outlines it. The imaging model rests on finer measurements of shape,
intensity and change, which depend on where the outline falls, and at MU it lost ground with Swin UNETR although that
network measured the enhancing volume more repeatably between close scans. Outlines of the same glioma differ between
experts \citep{hoebel2024}, and post-treatment scans are harder to segment than scans before surgery
\citep{deverdier2024}, so an image measurement is defined by its segmenter as much as by the tumour. PRISM records
which masks each measurement came from, and its report calls the volumes indicative. \todo{S4 PENDING: one sentence on
whether the post-treatment BraTS 2024 model narrows the MU imaging loss, written to match Section~\ref{sec:3.3}.}

% Sources: Sections 2.3 and 3.2; refs kallus2019, janes2008, beaulieujones2021.
Studies of progression models could report three things beside the AUC. The first is the gain over a
time-and-enhancement model, which shows what a score adds to the evidence a clinician already holds
\citep{beaulieujones2021}. The second is the AUC within time windows, the split of pairs within and across windows
\citep{kallus2019}, a pair weighted analogue of the covariate adjusted AUC \citep{janes2008}. The third is how the
result changes when another segmenter outlines the tumour. None of these needs new images, only the time of each scan
relative to treatment and a second public segmenter, and the first two need dates that most public glioma cohorts
record only in part.

% Sources: Introduction (jabal2026, herasrivera2026); Section 3.4; refs pham2026, mansour2026, mehrtens2023.
PRISM differs from the systems closest to it in where the clinical history enters. A system that chains language
models over clinical notes to assign a BT-RADS category \citep{jabal2026} reads the history as text, and a system that
phrases automated measurements with a language model \citep{herasrivera2026} reports the images. PRISM enters the
history as data in the record, so every sentence of its report can be checked against a value. Other rule-based gates
for clinical text forbid numbers altogether \citep{pham2026} or trace numbers to a measurement file without measuring
the check \citep{mansour2026}. Here the verifier rejected every planted value placed under the wrong region or score,
including those written by a model that had not seen its rules, and no correct paraphrase, while direction words
outside its fixed list remain for a wider list and a fresh set of independent errors. The conformal sets met their
average coverage at every centre on point estimates, but calibration within groups mainly redistributed coverage in cohorts of this
size, as expected under shift \citep{mehrtens2023}. Calibration pooled across centres would extend coverage to the
smallest groups, and a centre adopting PRISM would calibrate on its own scans.

% Sources: NUMBERS.md 10 (limits); Sections 2.1, 2.3; REPORT_V3_1.md Limits.
This study has limitations. PRISM was developed on one public cohort and tested at two external centres with 69 and
178 evaluation scans, so the external intervals are wide. The label is progression as each centre documented it, not
a pathological or expert consensus reference, and at LUMIERE and UCSF-ALPTDG it partly reflects the images the scores
use. The supplied masks and both segmenters give reference outlines that are not ground truth. At UCSF-ALPTDG the
radiotherapy window was approximated from its start, and the time-and-enhancement model was run there only in the
segmentation comparison. In the first 12 weeks after radiotherapy MU holds few scans with progression, and the report
marks such scans as having few comparable cases. No cohort supplied perfusion or diffusion imaging, which have
separated progression from treatment effects in other studies \citep{taylor2022}. No clinician has read the reports,
and the checks establish agreement with the record, not clinical value. Pooling rare situations across further
cohorts, such as Burdenko and UCSD-PTGBM, would sharpen these estimates, and a reader study of PRISM's reports is left
to future work.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

Without retraining, PRISM kept ranking scans at two external centres, and keeping its evidence separate showed what
that ranking rests on. The time since radiotherapy carried the ranking at the development centre and the enhancing
tumour at the external ones, and among scans taken at a similar time the images ranked progression at about 0.68.
PRISM shows each kind of evidence in its own score, and its local report states them, checked against the scan's
record.
